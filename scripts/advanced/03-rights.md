# equal-protection | IX. Equal Protection
@summary When government classifies, how closely courts scrutinize the classification.

## ep-framework | A. Framework
The Equal Protection Clause binds states. The Fifth Amendment's Due Process Clause imposes an identical equal protection requirement on the federal government (*Bolling v. Sharpe*, 1954, "reverse incorporation"). Every law classifies, so the question is which classifications demand justification.

!test Equal protection analysis
1. **Identify the classification.**
   - *Facial*: the law draws the line in its terms.
   - *Facially neutral* laws trigger heightened scrutiny only with proof of discriminatory **purpose**. Disparate impact alone is not enough (*Washington v. Davis*, 1976). Purpose means a decision made "because of, not merely in spite of" its effects (*Personnel Administrator v. Feeney*, 1979). Courts infer it from impact, history, procedural departures, and contemporaneous statements (*Arlington Heights*, 1977).
   - *Discriminatory application* of a neutral law (*Yick Wo v. Hopkins*, 1886).
2. **Choose the tier.** Strict scrutiny applies to race, national origin, alienage (state laws) and fundamental rights. Intermediate scrutiny applies to sex and nonmarital children. Rational basis applies to everything else.
3. **Apply the tier to ends and means.**
   - The government's interest must be compelling, important, or legitimate.
   - The fit must be narrow tailoring, a substantial relationship, or a rational relationship.
   - The burden is on the government under heightened scrutiny and on the challenger under rational basis.
!end

!cases
Washington v. Davis | 1976 | Disparate impact alone does not trigger heightened scrutiny.
Village of Arlington Heights v. Metropolitan Housing Development Corp. | 1977 | Factors for proving discriminatory purpose.
Yick Wo v. Hopkins | 1886 | Discriminatory administration of a neutral ordinance.
Bolling v. Sharpe | 1954 | Equal protection applies to the federal government through Fifth Amendment due process.
!end

!corner due-process, citizenship
!articles equal protection|discriminatory purpose|disparate impact

## strict | B. Strict scrutiny
Classifications based on **race** and **national origin** are inherently suspect, as are **state** classifications based on alienage (*Graham v. Richardson*, 1971). There are exceptions for "political functions" such as police officers and teachers (*Cabell v. Chavez-Salido*). Federal alienage rules get deference because of the immigration power.

The government must prove the classification is **narrowly tailored** to a **compelling** interest. Narrow tailoring requires necessity, serious consideration of race-neutral alternatives, a limited scope, and a logical end point. The tier is often described as "strict in theory, fatal in fact," though *Grutter* and *Korematsu* (later repudiated in *Trump v. Hawaii*) are exceptions.

**History.**
- *Dred Scott* (1857) denied citizenship to Black Americans.
- *Strauder v. West Virginia* (1880) struck down the exclusion of Black men from juries.
- *Plessy v. Ferguson* (1896) upheld "separate but equal."
- *Brown v. Board of Education* (1954) overruled *Plessy* in public education: "separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." *Brown II* (1955) ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed."
- *Loving v. Virginia* (1967) struck down interracial-marriage bans. Equal application to both races does not save a racial classification.

!cases
Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka (1) | 1954 | Racial segregation in public schools is unconstitutional.
Loving v. Virginia | 1967 | Antimiscegenation laws violate equal protection and due process.
Korematsu v. United States | 1944 | Japanese-American exclusion upheld (since repudiated).
Graham v. Richardson | 1971 | State alienage classifications are suspect.
!end

!excerpt Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka (1) | 1954 | in the field of public education the doctrine of separate but equal has no place
!corner slavery, citizenship, natural-rights
!articles strict scrutiny|segregation|brown v. board|racial classification

## intermediate | C. Intermediate scrutiny
**Sex.** Classifications based on sex must serve important governmental objectives and be substantially related to achieving them (*Craig v. Boren*, 1976). *United States v. Virginia* (1996) required an "exceedingly persuasive justification." The justification must be genuine, not invented for litigation, and must not rely on overbroad generalizations about men and women. *Frontiero v. Richardson* (1973) had a plurality for strict scrutiny but never a majority.
- Laws based on real biological differences have been upheld: statutory rape (*Michael M.*, 1981), citizenship transmission rules (*Nguyen v. INS*, 2001), and draft registration (*Rostker*, 1981).
- Sessions v. Morales-Santana (2017) struck a gender-based citizenship-transmission rule.

**Nonmarital children** ("illegitimacy"): classifications must be substantially related to an important interest (*Clark v. Jeter*, 1988).

**Transgender status.** *United States v. Skrmetti* (2025) upheld Tennessee's ban on certain medical treatments for transgender minors under rational-basis review. The majority characterized the law as classifying by age and medical use, not by sex or transgender status. The Court did not decide whether transgender status is a quasi-suspect class.

!cases
Craig v. Boren | 1976 | Intermediate scrutiny for sex classifications.
United States v. Virginia | 1996 | "Exceedingly persuasive justification" required; VMI's male-only admissions invalid.
Michael M. v. Superior Court of Sonoma County | 1981 | Sex-specific statutory rape law upheld.
!end

!excerpt Craig v. Boren | 1976 | classifications by gender must serve important governmental objectives and must be substantially related to achievement of those objectives
!excerpt United States v. Virginia | 1996 | exceedingly persuasive justification
!corner marriage-family
!articles sex discrimination|gender|intermediate scrutiny

## rational | D. Rational basis review
This is the default tier, applying to age (*Massachusetts Board of Retirement v. Murgia*, 1976), disability (*Cleburne*, 1985), wealth (*San Antonio v. Rodriguez*, 1973), and most economic classifications. The classification is valid if it is rationally related to a legitimate government interest. The challenger must negate "every conceivable basis" that might support it, and the legislature need not articulate its reasons (*FCC v. Beach Communications*, 1993).

**Rational basis "with bite."** Courts invalidate laws that rest on bare animus toward a politically unpopular group, which is not a legitimate interest.
- *Department of Agriculture v. Moreno* (1973) struck food stamp exclusions aimed at "hippies."
- *City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center* (1985) struck a permit denial for a group home based on fear of people with intellectual disabilities.
- *Romer v. Evans* (1996) struck a state constitutional amendment barring antidiscrimination protection for gay people.
- *United States v. Windsor* (2013) struck the federal DOMA definition of marriage.

!cases
City of Cleburne v. Cleburne Living Center, Inc. | 1985 | Animus-based permit denial fails rational basis.
Romer v. Evans | 1996 | Amendment born of animus toward a class fails rational basis.
Federal Communications Commission v. Beach Communications, Inc. | 1993 | Any conceivable rational basis suffices.
!end

!corner due-process
!articles rational basis|animus

## race-remedies | E. Race-conscious policies, remedies and other classifications
**All racial classifications get strict scrutiny,** whether "benign" or invidious and whether federal, state or local (*Richmond v. J.A. Croson*, 1989; *Adarand Constructors v. Peña*, 1995).
- **Remedial programs** require a strong basis in evidence of specific identified discrimination by the government unit. Societal discrimination does not suffice.
- **Higher education.** *Bakke* (1978) and *Grutter v. Bollinger* (2003) permitted race as a "plus" factor for diversity. *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* (2023) held that Harvard's and UNC's programs fail strict scrutiny: they lacked measurable objectives, used race negatively and as a stereotype, and had no logical end point. Applicants may still discuss how race affected their lives, so long as the institution treats each as an individual.
- **K-12 assignment.** *Parents Involved* (2007) struck racial balancing in student assignment.
- **Districting.** Race may not predominate in drawing districts without satisfying strict scrutiny (*Shaw v. Reno*, 1993; *Miller v. Johnson*, 1995). Compliance with Voting Rights Act § 2 has been assumed to be a compelling interest (*Allen v. Milligan*, 2023, applying *Gingles*). § 2 litigation is evolving; check the current Term.

**Sexual orientation.** The Court has not designated it a suspect class. Rights claims have succeeded under due process combined with equal protection (*Lawrence*, 2003; *Obergefell*, 2015).

!test Race-conscious government action (Croson / Adarand / SFFA)
1. Is there a racial classification, facial or purposeful? If so, strict scrutiny applies regardless of motive.
2. Compelling interest: remedying identified past discrimination by this actor, with a strong evidentiary basis. Diversity interests must be measurable.
3. Narrow tailoring: race-neutral alternatives considered; limited duration; no quotas; race never used as a negative or a stereotype.
4. Is there a logical end point?
!end

!cases
Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña | 1995 | Strict scrutiny for all federal racial classifications.
City of Richmond v. J. A. Croson Co. | 1989 | Strict scrutiny for state and local set-asides.
Grutter v. Bollinger | 2003 | Diversity rationale upheld (abrogated by SFFA).
Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College | 2023 | Race-conscious admissions programs fail strict scrutiny.
Shaw v. Reno | 1993 | Racial gerrymandering claims cognizable.
!end

!excerpt Adarand Constructors, Inc. v. Peña | 1995 | all racial classifications, imposed by whatever federal, state, or local governmental actor, must be analyzed by a reviewing court under strict scrutiny
!excerpt Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College | 2023 | Eliminating racial discrimination means eliminating all of it
!corner slavery, elections
!articles affirmative action|race-conscious|racial gerrymander|diversity

# fundamental-rights | X. Fundamental Rights
@summary Unenumerated liberties, the method for identifying them, and rights the Court treats as fundamental.

## identifying | A. Identifying fundamental rights
Substantive due process protects certain liberties from government interference regardless of the procedures used. When a law burdens a fundamental right, strict scrutiny applies. Other liberty interests get rational-basis review.

**Method.** *Washington v. Glucksberg* (1997) requires (1) a "careful description" of the asserted right and (2) proof that it is "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty." *Obergefell* (2015) described a more evolving, "reasoned judgment" approach for marriage. *Dobbs* (2022) reaffirmed *Glucksberg*'s history-and-tradition test as the governing method.

The level of generality matters enormously. Is the right asserted "the right to die," or "the right to refuse unwanted medical treatment"? Is it "the right to marry," or "the right to same-sex marriage"? Compare *Michael H. v. Gerald D.* (1989), footnote 6 (Scalia, J.): use "the most specific level at which a relevant tradition" exists.

!test Substantive due process
1. Carefully describe the asserted right.
2. Is it deeply rooted in history and tradition and implicit in ordered liberty? Or is it within a recognized precedent line (marriage, procreation, family, child-rearing, bodily integrity)?
3. If fundamental: strict scrutiny, meaning a compelling interest and narrowly tailored means.
4. If not: rational basis.
5. Consider equal protection as an alternative route when the law burdens the right unequally.
!end

!cases
Washington v. Glucksberg | 1997 | No fundamental right to assisted suicide; history-and-tradition method.
Obergefell v. Hodges | 2015 | Right to marry extends to same-sex couples.
!end

!excerpt Washington v. Glucksberg | 1997 | deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition
!corner due-process, natural-rights, common-law
!articles substantive due process|unenumerated|fundamental right

## privacy-family | B. Privacy, autonomy and family
**Family and child-rearing.** Parents have a fundamental right to direct the upbringing and education of their children: *Meyer v. Nebraska* (1923, foreign-language instruction), *Pierce v. Society of Sisters* (1925, private schools), and *Troxel v. Granville* (2000, grandparent visitation). The family includes extended family (*Moore v. City of East Cleveland*, 1977).

**Marriage.** *Loving* (1967) protected interracial marriage. *Zablocki v. Redhail* (1978) struck a requirement that fathers behind on child support obtain court permission to marry. *Turner v. Safley* (1987) protected prisoners' right to marry, and *Obergefell* (2015) extended the right to same-sex couples.

**Contraception and procreation.** *Skinner v. Oklahoma* (1942) struck compulsory sterilization of certain criminals on equal protection grounds. *Griswold v. Connecticut* (1965) protected married couples' use of contraception through "penumbras" of specific guarantees, and *Eisenstadt v. Baird* (1972) extended that to unmarried persons.

**Intimate conduct.** *Lawrence v. Texas* (2003) overruled *Bowers v. Hardwick* (1986) and struck criminal sodomy bans.

**Abortion.** *Roe v. Wade* (1973) recognized a right to abortion with a trimester framework. *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992) reaffirmed its "central holding" under an undue-burden standard. *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022) overruled both. It held that the Constitution confers no right to abortion because the right is not deeply rooted in history and tradition, and returned the issue to legislatures, where regulation receives rational-basis review. The majority stated that its decision does not cast doubt on other precedents. Justice Thomas's concurrence urged reconsidering all substantive due process precedents.

**Right to refuse treatment.** *Cruzan* (1990) assumed a competent person's right to refuse lifesaving treatment and permitted a clear-and-convincing evidence requirement. *Glucksberg* (1997) found no right to physician-assisted suicide.

!cases
Pierce v. Society of Sisters | 1925 | Parents may direct children's education, including private schooling.
Griswold v. Connecticut | 1965 | Married couples' right to use contraception.
Lawrence v. Texas | 2003 | Criminal bans on private intimate conduct unconstitutional.
Planned Parenthood of Southeastern Pennsylvania v. Casey | 1992 | Undue-burden standard (overruled).
Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization | 2022 | No constitutional right to abortion; Roe and Casey overruled.
!end

!excerpt Griswold v. Connecticut | 1965 | specific guarantees in the Bill of Rights have penumbras
!excerpt Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization | 2022 | The Constitution makes no reference to abortion, and no such right is implicitly protected by any constitutional provision
!corner life-unborn, marriage-family, due-process
!articles abortion|privacy|parental rights|marriage

## other-fundamental | C. Other fundamental-rights domains
**Right to travel.** *Saenz v. Roe* (1999) identified three components: (1) entering and leaving another state; (2) being treated as a welcome visitor, not a hostile alien (Article IV); and (3) for new permanent residents, being treated like longtime residents (Fourteenth Amendment Privileges or Immunities). Durational residency requirements for basic necessities fail (*Shapiro v. Thompson*, 1969; *Memorial Hospital v. Maricopa County*, 1974). A one-year requirement for divorce was upheld (*Sosna*).

**Voting.** Voting is "preservative of all rights."
- **One person, one vote** applies to congressional (*Wesberry*, 1964) and state legislative districts (*Reynolds v. Sims*, 1964). Total population is a permissible base (*Evenwel v. Abbott*, 2016).
- **Poll taxes** violate equal protection (*Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections*, 1966).
- **Election regulations** are reviewed under *Anderson–Burdick* balancing: severe burdens get strict scrutiny, and reasonable nondiscriminatory regulations need only important interests. Voter ID was upheld in *Crawford* (2008).
- **Bush v. Gore** (2000) held that standardless recounts violated equal protection.

**Access to courts.** There is no general right, but fees cannot bar indigents from divorce (*Boddie v. Connecticut*, 1971) or parental-termination appeals (*M.L.B. v. S.L.J.*, 1996). Criminal appeals as of right require counsel for indigents (*Douglas v. California*, 1963).

**Education.** *San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez* (1973) held there is no fundamental right to education and wealth is not a suspect class. *Plyler v. Doe* (1982) nonetheless struck Texas's exclusion of undocumented children, applying heightened rationality.

**Second Amendment.**
- *District of Columbia v. Heller* (2008) recognized an individual right to keep and bear arms, especially handguns in the home for self-defense. It noted "longstanding prohibitions" (felons, sensitive places) as presumptively lawful.
- *McDonald* (2010) incorporated it.
- *New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen* (2022) rejected means-end scrutiny and extended the right to public carry. When the text covers conduct, the government must show the regulation is consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation, through "relevantly similar" analogues judged by *how* and *why* they burden the right.
- *United States v. Rahimi* (2024) upheld disarming people subject to domestic-violence restraining orders. The historical inquiry seeks principles, not a "historical twin."

!test Second Amendment (Bruen / Rahimi)
1. Does the plain text cover the individual's conduct? Ask whether the person is among "the people," whether the item is an "arm," and whether the conduct is keeping or bearing.
2. If so, the conduct is presumptively protected.
3. The government must show the regulation is consistent with the principles underlying the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation:
   - compare *how* (the burden imposed) and *why* (the justification) with founding-era analogues;
   - 1791 is the primary reference point, and the 1868 evidence is debated;
   - analogues need not be "twins."
!end

!cases
Saenz v. Roe | 1999 | Three components of the right to travel.
Reynolds v. Sims | 1964 | One person, one vote for state legislatures.
Harper v. Virginia Board of Elections | 1966 | Poll taxes unconstitutional.
San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez | 1973 | No fundamental right to education.
District of Columbia v. Heller | 2008 | Individual right to keep and bear arms.
New York State Rifle & Pistol Association Inc. v. Bruen | 2022 | Text-and-history test; public carry protected.
United States v. Rahimi | 2024 | Disarming dangerous individuals consistent with tradition.
!end

!excerpt Reynolds v. Sims | 1964 | Legislators represent people, not trees or acres
!excerpt San Antonio Independent School District v. Rodriguez | 1973 | Education, of course, is not among the rights afforded explicit protection under our Federal Constitution
!excerpt District of Columbia v. Heller | 2008 | Like most rights, the right secured by the Second Amendment is not unlimited
!excerpt New York State Rifle & Pistol Association Inc. v. Bruen | 2022 | consistent with this Nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation
!corner arms, elections, citizenship, war

# speech | XI. First Amendment: Freedom of Expression
@summary The architecture of free-speech doctrine, from content discrimination and forums to the unprotected categories.

## speech-coverage | A. Coverage and government action
The First Amendment ("Congress shall make no law… abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press") applies to all government actors through incorporation (*Gitlow*, 1925). It does not apply to private actors: employers, private universities and platforms are not bound (*Manhattan Community Access v. Halleck*, 2019). Platforms' own editorial choices are themselves protected expression (*Moody v. NetChoice*, 2024).

!test First Amendment roadmap
1. Is there state action?
2. Is the activity speech or expressive conduct? Is it within an unprotected or less-protected category?
3. Is the regulation content-based (including viewpoint-based) or content-neutral?
4. Where does it occur? Apply forum analysis on government property.
5. Is there a special context: government employment, schools, government speech, subsidies, prisons, the military?
6. Apply the appropriate scrutiny and check vagueness and overbreadth.
7. Is it a prior restraint?
!end

**Overbreadth and vagueness.** A law that prohibits a substantial amount of protected speech relative to its legitimate sweep is facially invalid, even as applied to someone whose own speech is unprotected (*United States v. Stevens*, 2010; *United States v. Hansen*, 2023). Vague laws chill speech and invite arbitrary enforcement.

**Government speech.** When the government speaks, it may choose its message, so the Free Speech Clause does not apply (*Pleasant Grove City v. Summum*, 2009; *Walker v. Texas Division*, 2015, license plates; compare *Shurtleff v. Boston*, 2022, flag-raising program not government speech).

!cases
Gitlow v. New York | 1925 | Free speech applies to states.
United States v. Stevens | 2010 | Animal-cruelty depiction ban overbroad; no new categories by balancing.
Moody v. NetChoice, LLC | 2024 | Platforms' content moderation is protected editorial activity.
!end

## content | B. Content-based versus content-neutral regulation
**Content-based** laws target speech based on its communicative content: its topic, idea or message. They are presumptively unconstitutional and must satisfy strict scrutiny, meaning narrow tailoring to a compelling interest by the least restrictive means (*Reed v. Town of Gilbert*, 2015). A law is content-based on its face if it defines regulated speech by subject matter or by function or purpose. A facially neutral law is content-based if it cannot be justified without reference to content or was adopted because of disagreement with the message. Location-based sign rules are content-neutral even if they require reading the sign (*City of Austin v. Reagan National Advertising*, 2022).

**Viewpoint discrimination** is the most egregious form and is nearly always invalid, even in nonpublic forums and for trademarks (*Matal v. Tam*, 2017; *Iancu v. Brunetti*, 2019; *Rosenberger*, 1995).

**Content-neutral** time, place and manner rules are valid if they are narrowly tailored to a significant government interest and leave open ample alternative channels (*Ward v. Rock Against Racism*, 1989). Narrow tailoring here does *not* require the least restrictive means, only that the law not burden substantially more speech than necessary. *McCullen v. Coakley* (2014) struck down a thirty-five-foot abortion-clinic buffer zone as insufficiently tailored.

!cases
Reed v. Town of Gilbert | 2015 | Sign code distinguishing by subject matter is content-based.
Ward v. Rock Against Racism | 1989 | Time, place and manner test.
Matal v. Tam | 2017 | Disparagement bar on trademarks is viewpoint discrimination.
McCullen v. Coakley | 2014 | Clinic buffer zone not narrowly tailored.
!end

!excerpt Reed v. Town of Gilbert | 2015 | presumptively unconstitutional
!corner speech-press

## forums | C. Forum analysis
!test Public forum doctrine (Perry Education Ass'n, 1983)
1. **Traditional public forum** (streets, sidewalks, parks "time out of mind"):
   - content-based rules get strict scrutiny;
   - content-neutral rules get the time, place and manner test.
2. **Designated public forum** (property the government intentionally opens for expressive activity): the same as a traditional forum while it remains open.
3. **Limited public forum** (opened for particular subjects or speakers, such as student activity funds or school facilities after hours): restrictions must be reasonable in light of the forum's purpose and viewpoint neutral (*Rosenberger*; *Good News Club*, 2001).
4. **Nonpublic forum** (government property not open for expression, such as airport terminals in *ISKCON v. Lee*, 1992, and polling places in *Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky*, 2018): restrictions must be reasonable and viewpoint neutral. "Reasonable" requires objective, workable standards (*Mansky*).
!end

!cases
Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association | 1983 | Three-category forum framework.
Rosenberger v. Rector and Visitors of University of Virginia | 1995 | Viewpoint discrimination in a limited forum invalid.
Minnesota Voters Alliance v. Mansky | 2018 | Polling-place apparel ban unreasonable for lack of objective standards.
!end

!excerpt Perry Education Association v. Perry Local Educators' Association | 1983 | In places which by long tradition or by government fiat have been devoted to assembly and debate
!corner speech-press
!articles public forum|forum analysis

## prior-restraints | D. Prior restraints and licensing
Blackstone defined liberty of the press as freedom from prior restraints. Prior restraints, whether court injunctions or administrative censorship, bear a "heavy presumption" against validity.
- *Near v. Minnesota* (1931) struck an injunction against a "malicious, scandalous and defamatory" newspaper.
- *New York Times Co. v. United States* (1971, the Pentagon Papers) refused to enjoin publication of classified documents.
- *Nebraska Press Ass'n v. Stuart* (1976) struck a gag order on reporting about a criminal trial.

**Licensing schemes** must have narrow, objective, definite standards (*Shuttlesworth v. Birmingham*, 1969). Content-based licensing requires procedural safeguards: a brief period before a judicial determination, prompt judicial review, and a burden on the censor (*Freedman v. Maryland*, 1965). Permit fees may not vary with the anticipated hostility of the audience (*Forsyth County v. Nationalist Movement*, 1992).

!cases
Near v. Minnesota ex rel. Olson | 1931 | Injunction against newspaper publication is an invalid prior restraint.
New York Times Company v. United States | 1971 | Government failed to justify enjoining the Pentagon Papers.
!end

!excerpt New York Times Company v. United States | 1971 | Any system of prior restraints of expression comes to this Court bearing a heavy presumption against its constitutional validity
!corner speech-press, common-law
!articles prior restraint|pentagon papers|licensing

## categories | E. Unprotected and less-protected categories
Historically unprotected categories are "well-defined and narrowly limited" (*Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire*, 1942). Courts may not create new ones by balancing (*Stevens*; *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants*, 2011, violent video games; *United States v. Alvarez*, 2012, lies about military medals). Even within a category, viewpoint discrimination is forbidden (*R.A.V. v. St. Paul*, 1992).

!test Categories and their tests
1. **Incitement** (*Brandenburg v. Ohio*, 1969): advocacy directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it.
2. **True threats** (*Virginia v. Black*, 2003; *Counterman v. Colorado*, 2023): serious expressions of intent to commit unlawful violence against particular people. The speaker must at least have acted recklessly as to the threatening character.
3. **Fighting words** (*Chaplinsky*): face-to-face personal insults likely to provoke immediate violence. Narrowly construed today.
4. **Obscenity** (*Miller v. California*, 1973):
   - appeals to the prurient interest under contemporary community standards;
   - depicts sexual conduct in a patently offensive way as defined by state law;
   - taken as a whole, lacks serious literary, artistic, political or scientific value (judged by a national reasonable-person standard).
   Child pornography is unprotected without regard to *Miller* (*New York v. Ferber*, 1982), but virtual images are protected (*Ashcroft v. Free Speech Coalition*, 2002). Age-verification requirements for sexual content harmful to minors receive intermediate scrutiny (*Free Speech Coalition v. Paxton*, 2025).
5. **Defamation**:
   - Public officials and public figures must prove *actual malice*: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard (*New York Times v. Sullivan*, 1964; *Curtis Publishing v. Butts*, 1967).
   - Private figures need show only fault (at least negligence) for actual damages, and actual malice for presumed or punitive damages (*Gertz v. Robert Welch*, 1974).
   - For private figures on matters of private concern, the protections relax (*Dun & Bradstreet*, 1985).
   - Intentional infliction of emotional distress claims by public figures require actual malice (*Hustler v. Falwell*, 1988). Speech on public matters at a funeral is protected (*Snyder v. Phelps*, 2011).
6. **Commercial speech** (*Central Hudson*, 1980):
   - Is the speech about lawful activity and not misleading? (If not, it is unprotected.)
   - Is the government interest substantial?
   - Does the regulation directly advance that interest?
   - Is it not more extensive than necessary (a reasonable fit)?
   Content- and speaker-based burdens may get heightened review (*Sorrell v. IMS Health*, 2011).
!end

Miller v. California | 1973 | Three-part obscenity test.
New York Times Company v. Sullivan | 1964 | Actual malice standard for public officials.
Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc. | 1974 | Private-figure defamation standards.
Central Hudson Gas & Electric Corp. v. Public Service Commission of New York | 1980 | Commercial speech test.
!end

## special-contexts | F. Special contexts
**Symbolic conduct.** Conduct is expressive if there is an intent to convey a particularized message and a great likelihood it will be understood (*Spence v. Washington*; *Texas v. Johnson*, 1989, flag burning).

!test United States v. O'Brien (1968): regulation of conduct with expressive elements
1. The regulation is within the government's constitutional power.
2. It furthers an important or substantial government interest.
3. The interest is unrelated to the suppression of free expression. If it is related, apply strict scrutiny, as in *Texas v. Johnson*.
4. The incidental restriction on speech is no greater than essential to further that interest.
!end

**Public employees.**
- Speech as a citizen on matters of public concern is protected, subject to *Pickering* balancing against the employer's interest in efficient operations (*Pickering v. Board of Education*, 1968; *Connick v. Myers*, 1983).
- Speech made pursuant to official duties is unprotected (*Garcetti v. Ceballos*, 2006).
- Compelled union fees violate the First Amendment (*Janus*, 2018).
- Private religious expression by employees outside their duties is protected (*Kennedy v. Bremerton*, 2022).

**Students.** Students do not "shed their constitutional rights… at the schoolhouse gate." Schools may restrict speech that would materially and substantially disrupt (*Tinker v. Des Moines*, 1969). Further allowances cover lewd speech (*Fraser*), school-sponsored expression (*Hazelwood*), and promotion of illegal drugs (*Morse v. Frederick*). Off-campus speech receives more protection (*Mahanoy Area School District v. B.L.*, 2021).

**Campaign finance.** *Buckley v. Valeo* (1976) distinguished contributions, where limits are permissible to prevent quid pro quo corruption or its appearance, from expenditures, where limits get strict scrutiny and generally fail. *Citizens United v. FEC* (2010) struck limits on independent expenditures by corporations and unions. *McCutcheon* (2014) struck aggregate contribution limits, and *FEC v. Cruz* (2022) struck a limit on repaying candidate loans. Disclosure requirements receive "exacting scrutiny" (*Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, 2021, requiring narrow tailoring).

**Compelled speech and association.** The government may not compel affirmation of beliefs (*West Virginia v. Barnette*, 1943, the flag salute; *Wooley v. Maynard*, 1977), require speakers to host messages (*Hurley*, 1995), or compel the creation of expressive products contrary to the speaker's message (*303 Creative LLC v. Elenis*, 2023). Compelled factual disclosures in commercial advertising get relaxed review (*Zauderer*), but not in other settings (*NIFLA v. Becerra*, 2018). Expressive associations may exclude members when inclusion would significantly burden their message (*Boy Scouts v. Dale*, 2000).

# religion | XII. First Amendment: Religion
@summary Free exercise, establishment, and the space between them.

## free-exercise | A. Free Exercise
"Congress shall make no law… prohibiting the free exercise" of religion.

**The baseline.** *Employment Division v. Smith* (1990) held that a neutral, generally applicable law burdening religious practice (there, a peyote ban applied to Native American Church members) does not require strict scrutiny. This replaced the compelling-interest test of *Sherbert v. Verner* (1963) and *Wisconsin v. Yoder* (1972) outside the unemployment-compensation context.

**When strict scrutiny still applies.**
- **Lack of neutrality.** The law targets religion or shows hostility to it: *Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah* (1993, ordinances targeting animal sacrifice); *Masterpiece Cakeshop* (2018, commission hostility).
- **Lack of general applicability.** The law has a mechanism for individualized exemptions (*Fulton v. Philadelphia*, 2021), or treats any comparable secular activity more favorably than religious exercise. Comparability is judged by the risk the government interest addresses (*Tandon v. Newsom*, 2021).
- **Exclusion from public benefits** because of religious status or use violates free exercise (*Trinity Lutheran*, 2017; *Espinoza*, 2020; *Carson v. Makin*, 2022).
- **Hybrid rights** (*Smith* dicta), such as free exercise combined with parental rights. *Mahmoud v. Taylor* (2025) held that parents are entitled to notice and opt-outs where school instruction substantially interferes with their children's religious upbringing.
- **Church autonomy and the ministerial exception.** Religious institutions choose their ministers free of employment-discrimination law (*Hosanna-Tabor*, 2012; *Our Lady of Guadalupe*, 2020).

**Statutory protection.** The Religious Freedom Restoration Act (1993) restores strict scrutiny against the federal government (*Burwell v. Hobby Lobby*, 2014; *Tanzin v. Tanvir*, 2020, damages available). RLUIPA protects prisoners and land use (*Holt v. Hobbs*, 2015). Title VII requires accommodating employees' religion unless doing so imposes "substantial increased costs" (*Groff v. DeJoy*, 2023).

!test Free Exercise
1. Is a sincere religious exercise burdened?
2. Is the law neutral? Look for targeting, hostility, or a gerrymander.
3. Is it generally applicable? Look for individualized exemptions or comparable secular activity treated more favorably.
4. If either fails: strict scrutiny (compelling interest, narrowest means).
5. If both hold: rational basis under *Smith*. Then check RFRA (federal), RLUIPA, and state RFRAs.
6. Church autonomy or the ministerial exception may bar the claim entirely.
!end

!cases

!end

## establishment | B. The Establishment Clause
"Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion." *Everson v. Board of Education* (1947) incorporated the clause and invoked Jefferson's "wall of separation." *Lemon v. Kurtzman* (1971) required a secular purpose, a primary effect that neither advances nor inhibits religion, and no excessive entanglement; the endorsement test followed. *Kennedy v. Bremerton School District* (2022) declared *Lemon* and the endorsement test "abandoned." Courts must now interpret the clause "by reference to historical practices and understandings" (following *Town of Greece v. Galloway*, 2014, on legislative prayer, and *American Legion v. American Humanist Ass'n*, 2019, on the Bladensburg cross).

**What remains clearly forbidden:**
- **Coercion** of religious exercise, including formal prayer in public schools (*Engel v. Vitale*, 1962; *Abington v. Schempp*, 1963; *Lee v. Weisman*, 1992, graduation prayer; *Santa Fe v. Doe*, 2000, football games).
- **Denominational preferences**, which get strict scrutiny (*Larson v. Valente*, 1982; *Catholic Charities Bureau v. Wisconsin*, 2025).
- **Delegating governmental power** to religious bodies (*Larkin v. Grendel's Den*).
- Historical **hallmarks of establishment**: government control over doctrine and personnel, mandatory attendance, financial support of a church, using religious bodies for civil functions, punishing dissenters, and restricting worship.

**Funding.** Aid flowing to religious schools through genuine and independent private choice is permitted (*Zelman v. Simmons-Harris*, 2002). Once a state funds private schools, excluding religious ones violates free exercise (*Carson*). Direct aid must be neutral and secular in use (*Mitchell v. Helms*, 2000, plurality).

!test Establishment Clause after Kennedy v. Bremerton
1. Does the practice fit within historical practices and understandings at the founding (legislative prayer, ceremonial deism, longstanding monuments)?
2. Does it coerce participation in religious exercise? Coercion is especially sensitive with schoolchildren.
3. Does it prefer one denomination over others?
4. Does it bear the hallmarks of a founding-era establishment?
5. For funding: is aid neutral, and does it reach religious institutions through independent private choice?
!end

!end

## religion-interaction | C. How the clauses interact
The two clauses can pull against each other: accommodating religion may look like favoring it. The Court recognizes "play in the joints," a space where government may accommodate religion without being required to (*Walz*; *Locke v. Davey*, 2004, allowing exclusion of devotional theology degrees from scholarships, a holding later narrowed). Accommodations are permissible when they lift government-imposed burdens on religion and do not impose significant burdens on third parties (*Corporation of Presiding Bishop v. Amos*, 1987, Title VII exemption for religious employers; *Cutter v. Wilkinson*, 2005, RLUIPA). An accommodation that absolutely privileges religious interests over all others crosses the line (*Estate of Thornton v. Caldor*, 1985).

Religious institutions enjoy autonomy in matters of faith, doctrine and internal governance, including selecting ministers. Disputes over church property must be resolved under neutral principles of law, without deciding doctrinal questions (*Jones v. Wolf*, 1979).

!cases
Corporation of Presiding Bishop of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints v. Amos | 1987 | Religious-employer exemption from Title VII permissible accommodation.
Locke v. Davey | 2004 | "Play in the joints" between the clauses.
Cutter v. Wilkinson | 2005 | RLUIPA's prisoner provisions a permissible accommodation.
!end

# method | XIII. A Repeatable Constitutional Analysis Method
@summary An eight-step checklist for any constitutional question.

## checklist | The method
Work every constitutional problem through the same sequence. The early steps eliminate cases; the later ones decide them.

!test Eight steps
1. **Identify the actor and the action.** Federal, state or local? Legislative, executive or judicial? A statute, regulation, enforcement decision, or private conduct?
2. **Locate the provision and the type of claim.** Is it structural (enumerated powers, separation of powers, federalism, dormant Commerce Clause, preemption) or a rights claim (due process, equal protection, the First and Second Amendments, takings)? Many fact patterns raise both.
3. **Clear the thresholds.** Jurisdiction, standing, ripeness, mootness, political question, sovereign immunity, state action, and incorporation (for state actors).
4. **Choose the governing doctrine and level of scrutiny.** Name the test: *Lopez* categories, *Youngstown*, *Pike*, *Mathews*, *Penn Central*, *Central Hudson*, *Smith*/*Fulton*, *Bruen*, strict, intermediate or rational basis.
5. **State the government's interest and the record.** What does the government claim, and what evidence supports it? Under heightened scrutiny the justification must be genuine and contemporaneous.
6. **Apply every element.** Do not just name the tier. Work through ends, means, burden of proof, exceptions, and counterarguments on the facts.
7. **Step back.** Consider precedent (is the controlling case vulnerable?), interpretive method (would an originalist or living-constitutionalist analysis differ?), institutional competence, and remedy (severability; facial versus as-applied; injunction versus damages).
8. **Conclude narrowly.** Is it constitutional or not, on what ground, how far does the holding reach, and what relief is available?
!end

!corner judicial-review
!articles constitutional analysis|doctrine

# cross-cutting | XIV. High-Yield Cross-Cutting Principles
@summary Ideas that recur across every area of constitutional law.

## principles | Principles worth internalizing
- **Structure protects liberty.** Federalism and separation of powers are not just allocations of authority. They are designed to protect individuals by diffusing power (*Bond v. United States*, 2011: individuals may raise federalism claims; *Free Enterprise Fund*).
- **Rights generally require state action,** but Congress can reach private conduct through other powers, notably the Commerce Clause and the Thirteenth Amendment.
- **The level of scrutiny usually decides the case,** so the fight is often over classification and framing. Is the law sex-based or age-based? Is the right "marriage," or a more specific claim?
- **Facial versus as-applied.** A facial challenge must show the law is unconstitutional in all or a substantial number of its applications. That is the *Salerno* standard; overbreadth applies in speech cases (*Moody v. NetChoice*, 2024). As-applied challenges are narrower and favored.
- **Remedies are distinct from rights.**
  - *Severability*: courts excise the invalid provision if the rest can function (*Seila Law*; *Barr v. AAPC*, 2020).
  - Universal injunctions are generally unavailable (*Trump v. CASA*, 2025).
  - *Ex parte Young*, § 1983, and *Bivens* (now nearly closed to new contexts, *Egbert v. Boule*, 2022) define who can be sued for what.
- **Doctrine is historically contingent.** *Lemon*, *Chevron*, *Roe* and *Quill* all fell within a decade. Track controlling precedent and its subsequent treatment rather than memorizing tests as timeless.
- **Distinguish the layers.** Separate the constitutional rule, the statutory overlay (RFRA, the Voting Rights Act, Title VII, § 1983), and prudential or policy arguments.
- **Constitutional avoidance.** Courts construe statutes to avoid serious constitutional questions where fairly possible (*NFIB*; *Bond*), which often means the real constitutional question goes undecided.

!articles constitutional avoidance|facial challenge|severability|remedies
