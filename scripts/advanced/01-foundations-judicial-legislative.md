# foundations | I. Constitutional Foundations & Interpretation
@summary What the Constitution does, how its meaning is argued about, and why courts get to decide.

## functions | A. The Constitution's basic functions
A written constitution does four distinct jobs, and most constitutional disputes can be located by asking which job is at stake.

**It constitutes the government.** Articles I–III create Congress, the presidency and the federal judiciary, specify how officers are chosen, and prescribe the procedures by which each branch acts. Bicameralism and presentment (Art. I, § 7), the Appointments Clause (Art. II, § 2) and the "case or controversy" limit (Art. III, § 2) are procedural rules, not mere formalities. Many of the Court's separation-of-powers decisions turn on whether the prescribed procedure was followed.

**It allocates power.** Power is divided *horizontally* among three coequal branches, each with checks on the others, and *vertically* between the national government and the states. The national government is one of enumerated powers (Art. I, § 8; Tenth Amendment). The states retain a general "police power" to legislate for health, safety, welfare and morals.

**It constrains government.** The Bill of Rights, the Reconstruction Amendments, and the limits on states in Art. I, § 10 (no ex post facto laws, bills of attainder, or laws impairing contracts) protect individuals against the government.

**It entrenches higher law.** Because Article V makes amendment difficult, ordinary majorities cannot change constitutional rules by statute. That entrenchment is what makes judicial review consequential: a judicial ruling on constitutional meaning can be displaced only by amendment or by the Court itself.

!articles constitutional design|entrench|written constitution

## interpretation | B. Constitutional interpretation
Constitutional argument draws on a recognized set of "modalities" (the term is Philip Bobbitt's). A well-made argument names the modality it relies on and explains why it should control.

- **Text.** The words of the provision, read in context and with attention to their ordinary and legal meaning when adopted. Textual arguments are strongest where the text is specific (the President must be thirty-five) and weakest where it is open-textured ("due process," "unreasonable searches").
- **Original meaning and history.** Founding-era dictionaries, debates, ratification materials, and early practice. *Original public meaning* originalism asks what a reasonable reader at ratification would have understood; older *original intent* approaches asked what the drafters meant. The Court has increasingly relied on history and tradition, most prominently in *Heller*, *Bruen*, *Dobbs* and *Kennedy v. Bremerton*.
- **Structure.** Inferences from the relationships the Constitution sets up. Examples are federalism (*Printz*), separation of powers (*Chadha*), and the implied immunity of federal instrumentalities from state taxation (*McCulloch*).
- **Precedent.** Stare decisis promotes stability and reliance. The Court treats it as a "principle of policy," weakest in constitutional cases. When deciding whether to overrule, it weighs the quality of the reasoning, workability, consistency with related decisions, later developments, and reliance (*Dobbs*; *Janus*; *Ramos*).
- **Prudential and consequential arguments.** Administrability, institutional competence, and the practical effects of a rule.
- **Ethos and tradition.** Arguments from the nation's deeply rooted traditions or evolving moral commitments. These are most visible in substantive due process (*Glucksberg*, *Obergefell*), and their legitimacy is sharply contested.

The method a judge chooses often decides the case. In *Dobbs* the majority's history-and-tradition test, applied to abortion, produced the opposite answer from the dissent's precedent-and-liberty analysis.

!test Overruling precedent: factors the Court considers
1. The nature of the error: how wrong was the decision?
2. The quality of the reasoning.
3. Workability of the rule it established.
4. Effect on other areas of law (doctrinal coherence).
5. Reliance interests, especially concrete property and contract reliance.
!end

!end

## supremacy-review | C. Judicial role and constitutional supremacy
The Supremacy Clause (Art. VI, cl. 2) makes the Constitution, federal statutes made "in pursuance thereof," and treaties "the supreme Law of the Land," binding on state judges. *Marbury v. Madison* (1803) drew the further inference that courts deciding cases must refuse to enforce statutes inconsistent with the Constitution. A written constitution limiting legislative power would be pointless if the legislature could override it, and judges take an oath to support the Constitution.

**Judicial review and judicial supremacy are different claims.** Judicial review is the power to disregard unconstitutional laws when deciding a case. Judicial supremacy is the stronger claim that the Court's interpretations bind the other branches and the states generally. *Cooper v. Aaron* (1958) asserted supremacy against Arkansas's resistance to *Brown*, and *City of Boerne v. Flores* (1997) invoked it against Congress. Departmentalists (in the tradition of Jefferson, Jackson and Lincoln) argue each branch interprets the Constitution for itself. Popular constitutionalists argue the people retain final authority.

**Limits come with the role.** Courts speak only in justiciable cases (Part II), only to the extent their remedial authority allows, and only on the questions presented. In *Trump v. CASA, Inc.* (2025) the Court held that federal courts generally lack equitable authority to issue "universal" injunctions protecting non-parties.

!cases
Marbury v. Madison | 1803 | Courts may refuse to apply statutes that conflict with the Constitution.
Cooper v. Aaron | 1958 | State officials are bound by the Court's constitutional interpretations.
!end

!excerpt Marbury v. Madison | 1803 | It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is
!excerpt Cooper v. Aaron | 1958 | the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution

# judicial | II. Federal Judicial Power
@summary Who may sue, when, about what, and how far Congress and state sovereignty limit the federal courts.

## review-of-states | A. Judicial review of state and federal action
Section 25 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 gave the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction over state high courts that rejected federal claims. *Martin v. Hunter's Lessee* (1816) upheld that review of state courts in civil cases, and *Cohens v. Virginia* (1821) extended it to criminal cases. The Court reasoned that national uniformity in federal law requires a single final interpreter.

**Adequate and independent state grounds.** The Court reviews only federal questions. If a state-court judgment rests on a state-law ground that is *adequate* (it alone supports the judgment) and *independent* (not intertwined with federal law), the Court will not review it, because any federal ruling would be advisory. When a state opinion is ambiguous, *Michigan v. Long* (1983) presumes the decision rests on federal law unless the state court makes a "plain statement" that state law independently supports it.

!test Adequate and independent state ground
1. Does the judgment rest on a state-law ground that would sustain it regardless of the federal issue? If so, it is adequate.
2. Is that ground free of reliance on federal law? If so, it is independent.
3. If the opinion is ambiguous, presume federal reliance unless there is a clear statement otherwise (*Michigan v. Long*).
!end

!cases
Martin v. Hunter's Lessee | 1816 | Supreme Court may review state-court judgments on federal questions.
Michigan v. Long | 1983 | "Plain statement" rule for adequate and independent state grounds.
!end

## advisory | B1. No advisory opinions
Article III extends judicial power only to "Cases" and "Controversies." From the beginning the Justices declined to give the executive legal advice. In 1793, Chief Justice Jay politely refused Secretary of State Jefferson's request for guidance on neutrality questions. *Hayburn's Case* (1792) involved circuit-riding Justices refusing to serve as pension commissioners whose decisions the Secretary of War could revise. It established that federal court judgments cannot be subject to revision by the political branches. That principle was reaffirmed in *Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm* (1995), which barred Congress from reopening final judgments.

A justiciable controversy requires (1) actual adverse parties, (2) a real dispute rather than a hypothetical, and (3) a likelihood that the court's decision will have practical legal effect. Declaratory judgments are permissible when they resolve an actual controversy between parties with adverse legal interests.

!cases
Plaut v. Spendthrift Farm, Inc. | 1995 | Congress cannot require federal courts to reopen final judgments.
!end

!articles advisory opinion|case or controversy

## standing | B2. Standing
Standing asks whether *this plaintiff* is the proper party to bring *this claim*. The "irreducible constitutional minimum," as framed in *Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife* (1992), has three elements.

**Injury in fact.** The harm must be concrete, particularized, and actual or imminent. *Concrete* means real, not abstract; intangible harms qualify if they bear a close relationship to harms traditionally recognized at common law (*Spokeo*; *TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez*, 2021). A bare statutory violation is not enough. *Particularized* means it affects the plaintiff in a personal and individual way. Future injury must be "certainly impending" or present a "substantial risk" (*Clapper*; *Susan B. Anthony List*).

**Causation (traceability).** The injury must be fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct, not the independent action of a third party not before the court. Standing is hardest to establish when the harm runs through third parties' choices (*Murthy v. Missouri*, 2024; *FDA v. Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine*, 2024).

**Redressability.** A favorable decision must be likely, not merely speculative, to redress the injury.

**Prudential and related limits.**
- *Third-party standing* is generally barred, but allowed where the litigant has a close relationship with the right-holder and there is a hindrance to the right-holder's own suit (e.g., doctors for patients; vendors for customers, *Craig v. Boren*).
- *Generalized grievances* shared by all citizens (e.g., an interest in lawful government) are not cognizable. Taxpayer standing exists only under the narrow *Flast v. Cohen* exception for Establishment Clause challenges to congressional spending, which *Hein* (2007) and *Arizona Christian School Tuition Org. v. Winn* (2011) confined.
- *Zone of interests* asks whether a statutory claim falls within the interests the statute protects. *Lexmark* (2014) recast this as a question of statutory interpretation.
- *Organizational standing* exists either for injury to the organization itself or as associational standing on behalf of members.
- *State standing* includes sovereign, quasi-sovereign and proprietary interests (*Massachusetts v. EPA*, 2007, "special solicitude"; compare *United States v. Texas*, 2023, and *Biden v. Nebraska*, 2023).

Standing is assessed plaintiff by plaintiff, claim by claim, and form of relief by form of relief (*Lyons*: a past injury supports damages but not an injunction absent a real threat of repetition).

!test Constitutional standing (Lujan)
1. Injury in fact: concrete, particularized, actual or imminent.
2. Causation: fairly traceable to the defendant's challenged conduct.
3. Redressability: likely to be redressed by a favorable decision.
4. Check the prudential overlays: third-party standing, generalized grievance, zone of interests.
5. Repeat for each claim and each form of relief sought.
!end

!cases

City of Los Angeles v. Lyons | 1983 | Injunctive relief requires a real and immediate threat of future injury.
Massachusetts v. Environmental Protection Agency | 2007 | States receive "special solicitude" in the standing analysis.
TransUnion LLC v. Ramirez | 2021 | Concreteness requires a close analogue to traditionally recognized harms.
!end

## ripeness | B3. Ripeness
Ripeness prevents courts from deciding disputes too early, before the facts or the legal question have developed enough. Under *Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner* (1967), courts weigh (1) the **fitness of the issues** for judicial decision (is the question purely legal? is the agency action final?) and (2) the **hardship to the parties** of withholding review (must the plaintiff choose between costly compliance and risking sanctions?).

**Pre-enforcement challenges.** A plaintiff need not violate a law and await prosecution to challenge it. It suffices to allege "an intention to engage in a course of conduct arguably affected with a constitutional interest, but proscribed by a statute," with "a credible threat of prosecution" (*Babbitt v. United Farm Workers*; *Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus*, 2014). Ripeness and standing overlap heavily in this setting. *Whole Woman's Health v. Jackson* (2021) illustrates the limits: for laws enforced only through private suits, there may be no state officer to enjoin.

!test Ripeness
1. Fitness: is the issue purely legal and the challenged action final, or does it depend on contingent future events?
2. Hardship: does delaying review impose a direct and immediate dilemma on the plaintiff?
3. Pre-enforcement: is there an intention to engage in arguably protected conduct, which is proscribed, with a credible threat of enforcement?
!end

!cases
Abbott Laboratories v. Gardner | 1967 | Fitness-plus-hardship test for ripeness.
Susan B. Anthony List v. Driehaus | 2014 | Credible threat of enforcement supports pre-enforcement review.
!end

!articles ripeness|pre-enforcement

## mootness | B4. Mootness
A case must remain live at every stage of litigation, not just when filed. Mootness is sometimes described as "standing set in a time frame." The exceptions matter more than the rule.

- **Collateral consequences.** A criminal conviction remains challengeable after the sentence is served because of its continuing legal disabilities (*Sibron v. New York*).
- **Capable of repetition, yet evading review.** The challenged action is too short in duration to be fully litigated, and there is a reasonable expectation the same complaining party will be subject to it again. The classic example is pregnancy in *Roe v. Wade*; elections and short-term orders are others.
- **Voluntary cessation.** A defendant's voluntary halt to challenged conduct moots a case only if it is "absolutely clear the allegedly wrongful behavior could not reasonably be expected to recur" (*Friends of the Earth v. Laidlaw*, 2000). Government defendants get somewhat more benefit of the doubt, but *West Virginia v. EPA* (2022) and *FBI v. Fikre* (2024) applied the doctrine to the government.
- **Class actions.** A properly certified class retains a live controversy even if the named plaintiff's claim becomes moot (*Sosna v. Iowa*).

!cases
Friends of the Earth, Inc. v. Laidlaw Environmental Services, Inc. | 2000 | Voluntary cessation does not moot a case unless recurrence is absolutely clear not to occur.
DeFunis v. Odegaard | 1974 | Challenge moot where the plaintiff was about to graduate.
!end

!articles mootness|capable of repetition

## political-question | B5. The political question doctrine
Some constitutional questions are committed to the political branches or cannot be resolved with judicial standards. *Baker v. Carr* (1962) listed six indicators, and the first two do nearly all the work in modern cases.

!test Baker v. Carr political question factors
1. A textually demonstrable constitutional commitment of the issue to a coordinate political department.
2. A lack of judicially discoverable and manageable standards for resolving it.
3. The impossibility of deciding without an initial policy determination of a kind clearly for nonjudicial discretion.
4. The impossibility of a court's independent resolution without expressing lack of the respect due coordinate branches.
5. An unusual need for unquestioning adherence to a political decision already made.
6. The potential embarrassment from multifarious pronouncements by various departments on one question.
!end

**Applications.**
- **Guarantee Clause** claims are classically nonjusticiable (*Luther v. Borden*, 1849).
- **Senate impeachment trial procedures** are textually committed to the Senate, which has the "sole Power to try" impeachments (*Nixon v. United States*, 1993).
- **Partisan gerrymandering** claims present political questions beyond the reach of federal courts for lack of manageable standards (*Rucho v. Common Cause*, 2019). Racial gerrymandering and one-person-one-vote claims remain justiciable (*Baker*; *Reynolds*).
- **Foreign affairs** cases are not automatically political. In *Zivotofsky v. Clinton* (2012), whether a statute about passport birthplaces was constitutional was a familiar question of statutory and constitutional interpretation.

!cases
Baker v. Carr | 1962 | Malapportionment claims justiciable; six-factor test.
Nixon v. United States | 1993 | Senate's impeachment-trial procedures are nonjusticiable.
Rucho v. Common Cause | 2019 | Partisan gerrymandering claims are political questions.
Zivotofsky v. Clinton | 2012 | Courts can decide the constitutionality of a passport statute.
!end

!excerpt Baker v. Carr | 1962 | Prominent on the surface of any case held to involve a political question is found a textually demonstrable constitutional commitment

## jurisdiction-control | C. Congressional control of federal jurisdiction
Article III vests judicial power in "one supreme Court" and "such inferior Courts as the Congress may from time to time ordain and establish." Article III also gives the Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction "with such Exceptions, and under such Regulations as the Congress shall make." Under the traditional "Madisonian compromise," Congress could have created no lower federal courts at all, so it may define their jurisdiction narrowly (*Sheldon v. Sill*, 1850).

**Exceptions to Supreme Court appellate jurisdiction.** In *Ex parte McCardle* (1869), Congress repealed the statute under which a pending habeas appeal had been filed, and the Court dismissed. It noted, however, that other routes to review remained (*Ex parte Yerger*). The outer limits remain unsettled. Scholars debate whether Congress may strip *all* federal jurisdiction over a constitutional claim (Henry Hart's "essential functions" thesis).

**Limits that are settled.**
- Congress may not use jurisdictional rules to dictate the outcome of a particular case under existing law (*United States v. Klein*, 1872). Changing the underlying law and applying it to pending cases is permitted (*Bank Markazi v. Peterson*, 2016; *Patchak v. Zinke*, 2018).
- Congress may not reopen final judgments (*Plaut*).
- Jurisdictional restrictions may not themselves violate other provisions: equal protection, due process, or the Suspension Clause (*Boumediene v. Bush*, 2008).
- Congress may assign some matters to non-Article III tribunals ("public rights"), but private-rights claims of the kind traditionally tried at common law require an Article III court (*Stern v. Marshall*, 2011; *Oil States*, 2018). When such claims seek civil penalties resembling common-law suits, the Seventh Amendment requires a jury (*SEC v. Jarkesy*, 2024).

!cases
Ex parte McCardle | 1869 | Congress may withdraw appellate jurisdiction over a pending appeal.
United States v. Klein | 1872 | Congress may not prescribe a rule of decision in pending cases.
Boumediene v. Bush | 2008 | Suspension Clause limits Congress's power to strip habeas jurisdiction.
SEC v. Jarkesy | 2024 | Civil-penalty fraud claims require a jury trial in an Article III court.
!end

!excerpt Ex parte McCardle | 1869 | Without jurisdiction the court cannot proceed at all in any cause

## sovereign-immunity | D. State sovereign immunity
The Eleventh Amendment was adopted in 1795 to overturn *Chisholm v. Georgia* (1793), which had allowed a citizen of South Carolina to sue Georgia. Its text bars only suits against a state by citizens of *another* state or a foreign state. *Hans v. Louisiana* (1890) held, however, that states also enjoy immunity from suits by their *own* citizens, because the Amendment confirms a broader background principle of sovereign immunity. *Alden v. Maine* (1999) extended that immunity to private federal claims in state courts.

**Routes around immunity.**
- **Consent and waiver.** States may consent to suit, but waiver must be unequivocally expressed.
- **Congressional abrogation.** Congress must use clear statutory language and act under a valid power. It may abrogate under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment (*Fitzpatrick v. Bitzer*, 1976), subject to congruence and proportionality. It may not abrogate under Article I powers such as the Commerce Clause (*Seminole Tribe v. Florida*, 1996). Bankruptcy is an exception rooted in the plan of the Convention (*Central Virginia Community College v. Katz*, 2006). States also consented in the plan of the Convention to suits involving federal eminent domain and war powers (*PennEast*, 2021; *Torres v. Texas DPS*, 2022).
- **Ex parte Young (1908).** A suit against a state *officer* for prospective injunctive relief to stop an ongoing violation of federal law is not a suit against the state. Retrospective monetary relief payable from the state treasury is barred (*Edelman v. Jordan*, 1974).
- **Suits by the United States or by other states** are not barred.
- **Local governments** (counties, cities) have no Eleventh Amendment immunity, though § 1983 liability requires an official policy or custom (*Monell*). Individual officers sued for damages in their personal capacity may raise qualified immunity, a separate doctrine.

!test Is a suit against a state barred?
1. Is the defendant the state or an arm of the state, rather than a municipality or an officer sued personally?
2. Did the state unequivocally waive immunity?
3. Did Congress clearly abrogate immunity under a valid power (Fourteenth Amendment § 5; bankruptcy)?
4. Is this an *Ex parte Young* suit for prospective relief against an officer?
5. Is the plaintiff the United States or another state?
!end

!cases
Hans v. Louisiana | 1890 | States are immune from suits by their own citizens.
Ex parte Young | 1908 | Officers may be enjoined from enforcing unconstitutional state laws.
Seminole Tribe of Florida v. Florida | 1996 | Congress cannot abrogate state immunity under Article I.
Alden v. Maine | 1999 | State immunity extends to federal claims in state court.
!end

!corner federalism, judicial-review
!articles sovereign immunity|eleventh amendment|ex parte young

# legislative | III. Federal Legislative Power
@summary Congress has only the powers the Constitution grants. Here is how far each one reaches.

## enumerated | A. Enumerated powers and necessary-and-proper means
The first question about any federal statute is "Where does Congress get the power?" Congress has no general police power. *United States v. Lopez* (1995) and *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) both stressed that reading any power so broadly as to supply one would collapse the distinction "between what is truly national and what is truly local."

**The Necessary and Proper Clause** (Art. I, § 8, cl. 18) lets Congress choose the means for executing its enumerated powers. In *McCulloch v. Maryland* (1819), Chief Justice Marshall read "necessary" to mean "convenient" or "useful," not "absolutely necessary." Congress could therefore charter a national bank as a means to its taxing, borrowing and war powers. *United States v. Comstock* (2010) upheld civil commitment of sexually dangerous federal prisoners as a "rational" means "reasonably adapted" to legitimate ends. *NFIB* added that a law must also be "proper," meaning consistent with the Constitution's structure. A law that creates a new, independent power (such as compelling commerce) is not merely incidental.

**Supremacy.** Valid federal law displaces conflicting state law (see Preemption, Part V). *McCulloch* also held that states may not tax federal instrumentalities, because "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."

!test Is a federal statute within Congress's power?
1. Identify the enumerated power relied on (commerce, tax, spending, § 5, war, and so on).
2. Is the statute itself an exercise of that power?
3. If not, is it a necessary and proper means: rationally related and reasonably adapted to executing that power?
4. Is it "proper": does it respect federalism and other structural limits (anti-commandeering, sovereign immunity)?
5. Does it violate any independent constitutional limit, such as an individual right?
!end

!cases
McCulloch v. Maryland | 1819 | Broad reading of the Necessary and Proper Clause; states may not tax the national bank.
United States v. Comstock | 2010 | Civil commitment of federal prisoners is a necessary and proper means.
!end

!excerpt McCulloch v. Maryland | 1819 | Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution
!corner necessary-proper, federalism, money
!articles necessary and proper|enumerated powers|implied power

## commerce | B. The Commerce Clause
Congress may "regulate Commerce with foreign Nations, and among the several States, and with the Indian Tribes." The clause has gone through four eras.

1. **Gibbons v. Ogden (1824).** Commerce is "intercourse," including navigation, and Congress's power over it is plenary within its sphere.
2. **1887–1937: formal limits.** The Court distinguished commerce from "manufacturing," "mining" and "production" (*E.C. Knight*, 1895) and "direct" from "indirect" effects (*Schechter Poultry*, 1935). In *Hammer v. Dagenhart* (1918) it struck down a child-labor law aimed at production.
3. **1937–1995: near-plenary power.** *NLRB v. Jones & Laughlin Steel* (1937), *United States v. Darby* (1941) and *Wickard v. Filburn* (1942) upheld regulation of activities with a "substantial economic effect" on interstate commerce, aggregated across all similarly situated actors. The Civil Rights Act of 1964 was sustained under this power (*Heart of Atlanta Motel*; *Katzenbach v. McClung*).
4. **1995–present: outer limits.** *Lopez* struck down the Gun-Free School Zones Act, and *United States v. Morrison* (2000) struck down the civil remedy in the Violence Against Women Act. *Gonzales v. Raich* (2005) nonetheless upheld federal prohibition of home-grown medical marijuana as part of a comprehensive scheme regulating a national market. In *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012), five Justices concluded the clause does not authorize compelling individuals to *engage* in commerce by buying insurance.

!test Modern Commerce Clause categories (Lopez)
1. **Channels** of interstate commerce: highways, waterways, airspace, and the movement of goods and people through them.
2. **Instrumentalities** of interstate commerce, and persons or things in interstate commerce, even when the threat comes from intrastate activity.
3. **Activities that substantially affect** interstate commerce. Here the Court asks:
   - Is the regulated activity economic in nature? If so, aggregation is permitted (*Wickard*; *Raich*).
   - Does the statute have a jurisdictional element tying it to interstate commerce?
   - Are there congressional findings? Relevant, but not dispositive (*Morrison*).
   - How attenuated is the link between the activity and the effect on commerce?
4. Congress may not compel individuals to enter commerce (*NFIB*, five Justices).
!end

**The anti-commandeering principle.** Congress may regulate private actors directly, preempt state law, and offer states incentives. It may not commandeer state legislatures or executive officers.
- *New York v. United States* (1992) struck the "take title" provision for radioactive waste.
- *Printz v. United States* (1997) held that the Brady Act could not conscript local sheriffs to run background checks.
- *Murphy v. NCAA* (2018) held that Congress may not forbid states from repealing their own laws, so a ban on state authorization of sports gambling was invalid.

The doctrine does not bar generally applicable laws that regulate states as actors (*Reno v. Condon*, 2000), conditional preemption, or information-reporting duties in some contexts.

Heart of Atlanta Motel, Inc. v. United States | 1964 | Civil Rights Act's public-accommodation provisions sustained.
United States v. Lopez | 1995 | Gun possession near schools is not economic activity substantially affecting commerce.
Gonzales v. Raich | 2005 | Congress may prohibit intrastate marijuana as part of a national market scheme.
New York v. United States | 1992 | Congress may not commandeer state legislatures.
Printz v. United States | 1997 | Congress may not commandeer state executive officers.
!end

## tax-spend | C. Taxing and spending powers
**Taxing power.** Congress may "lay and collect Taxes, Duties, Imposts and Excises, to pay the Debts and provide for the common Defence and general Welfare." Taxes are judged by function, not label. In *NFIB v. Sebelius* (2012) the individual-mandate "penalty" was upheld as a tax because it was modest, had no scienter requirement, and was collected by the IRS through normal means. By contrast, a "tax" that operates as a penalty for unlawful conduct, with a heavy exaction and a scienter requirement, exceeds the power (*Child Labor Tax Case*, 1922). Direct taxes must be apportioned among the states by population unless they are income taxes (Sixteenth Amendment). *Moore v. United States* (2024) upheld a tax on undistributed income of foreign corporations attributed to shareholders.

**Spending power.** *United States v. Butler* (1936) adopted Hamilton's view: Congress may spend for the general welfare, not merely to carry out other enumerated powers. Conditions on grants to states are permitted within limits.

!test Conditional spending (South Dakota v. Dole; NFIB)
1. The spending must be in pursuit of the general welfare (courts defer heavily).
2. Conditions must be stated unambiguously, so states can accept knowingly (*Pennhurst*).
3. Conditions must be related to the federal interest in the particular program.
4. Conditions may not induce states to violate other constitutional provisions.
5. The financial inducement may not be so coercive that "pressure turns into compulsion." In *NFIB*, threatening all existing Medicaid funding to force the expansion was "a gun to the head."
!end

!cases
United States v. Butler | 1936 | Spending power extends to the general welfare (Hamiltonian view).
South Dakota v. Dole | 1987 | Highway funds conditioned on drinking age of 21 upheld.
National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius | 2012 | Mandate upheld as tax; Medicaid expansion condition coercive.
!end

!excerpt National Federation of Independent Business v. Sebelius | 2012 | gun to the head

## section-five | D. Reconstruction Amendments and congressional enforcement
Section 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment (with § 2 of the Thirteenth and Fifteenth) gives Congress power to "enforce" the amendment "by appropriate legislation."

**Remedy, not redefinition.** *City of Boerne v. Flores* (1997) held that Congress may enact prophylactic legislation reaching some constitutional conduct to deter or remedy violations. It may not change the substantive meaning of the amendment. The Religious Freedom Restoration Act, which tried to restore strict scrutiny after *Employment Division v. Smith*, exceeded § 5 as applied to the states (it still binds the federal government).

**Congruence and proportionality.** Courts compare the scope of the remedy to a record of constitutional violations.
- Laws protecting classes that receive only rational-basis review generally fail: age discrimination in *Kimel* (2000) and Title I of the ADA (disability, employment) in *Garrett* (2001).
- Laws addressing heightened-scrutiny classifications or fundamental rights fare better: the FMLA family-leave provision (sex discrimination) in *Hibbs* (2003), and Title II of the ADA as applied to access to courts in *Tennessee v. Lane* (2004).

**Private conduct.** Section 5 reaches state action, not private conduct (*Civil Rights Cases*, 1883; *United States v. Morrison*, 2000). The Thirteenth Amendment, by contrast, reaches private "badges and incidents of slavery" (*Jones v. Alfred H. Mayer Co.*, 1968).

**Fifteenth Amendment.** *South Carolina v. Katzenbach* (1966) upheld the Voting Rights Act's preclearance regime under a "rational means" standard. *Shelby County v. Holder* (2013) held the coverage formula unconstitutional because it rested on decades-old data, invoking the "equal sovereignty" of the states.

**Section 3.** In *Trump v. Anderson* (2024), the Court held that states may not enforce Section 3's insurrection disqualification against federal officeholders and candidates; enforcement rests with Congress.

!test Section 5 enforcement legislation (Boerne)
1. Identify the constitutional right Congress is enforcing, as the Court has defined it.
2. Examine the legislative record of constitutional violations by states.
3. Ask whether the remedy is congruent and proportional to that pattern of violations.
4. For damages claims against states, confirm clear abrogation of sovereign immunity.
!end

## other-powers | E. Other powers
- **Bankruptcy** (uniform laws): includes a limited exception to state sovereign immunity (*Katz*). Uniformity forbids private-debtor-specific laws (*Railway Labor Executives' Ass'n v. Gibbons*, 1982).
- **Naturalization**: Congress has plenary authority over the rules for citizenship by naturalization, and broad immigration power as an incident of sovereignty (*Chae Chan Ping*, 1889). Birthright citizenship flows from the Fourteenth Amendment's Citizenship Clause (*Wong Kim Ark*, 1898), not Article I.
- **Intellectual property**: patents and copyrights "for limited Times" to "promote the Progress of Science and useful Arts." Congress gets wide latitude on duration (*Eldred v. Ashcroft*, 2003, upholding the twenty-year copyright extension).
- **War and military powers**: declaring war, raising armies, regulating the land and naval forces, calling forth the militia. These support broad wartime economic regulation (*Woods v. Cloyd W. Miller Co.*, 1948, rent control after the war) and military justice systems.
- **Treaty power**: treaties may address matters beyond Article I, but must be construed to avoid displacing traditional state criminal law absent a clear statement (*Bond v. United States*, 2014). Treaties cannot override constitutional rights (*Reid v. Covert*, 1957).
- **Property and territory**: Congress may "make all needful Rules and Regulations respecting the Territory or other Property belonging to the United States" (Art. IV, § 3). The *Insular Cases* doctrine governing unincorporated territories remains in force, though widely criticized.
- **Investigations**: an implied power auxiliary to legislation, limited to valid legislative purposes. Subpoenas for a President's personal records receive heightened scrutiny (*Trump v. Mazars*, 2020).

Federalism principles constrain even valid exercises of these powers. Anti-commandeering and state sovereign immunity apply regardless of which power Congress invokes.

!end
