# Beginner's Guide quizzes. Format:
#   ## guide:<topic-id>
#   Q: question
#   - wrong answer
#   * correct answer
#   E: explanation shown after answering
# Wrong answers are written to be about as long and as specific as the right one, so length never gives it away.

## guide:judicial-review
Q: Which case first established that federal courts may refuse to enforce a statute that conflicts with the Constitution?
- McCulloch v. Maryland
* Marbury v. Madison
- Cooper v. Aaron
- Gibbons v. Ogden
E: In Marbury v. Madison (1803), Chief Justice Marshall reasoned that a written constitution limiting Congress would be meaningless if courts had to enforce laws that violate it.

Q: Where does the text of the Constitution expressly grant courts the power of judicial review?
- Article III, Section 1, which vests "the judicial Power" in one Supreme Court and the lower courts Congress creates
- The Supremacy Clause of Article VI, which tells judges to treat the Constitution as the supreme law of the land
- Article III, Section 2, which extends the judicial power to all cases "arising under this Constitution"
* Nowhere; judicial review is inferred from the Constitution's structure and the judicial role
E: Judicial review is not spelled out in the text. Marbury inferred it from the written Constitution, the Supremacy Clause, the judicial oath and the nature of judging.

Q: In Cooper v. Aaron (1958), what did the Court tell Arkansas officials resisting school desegregation?
- That Brown v. Board bound only the school boards that were parties to it
* That state officials are bound by the Supreme Court's interpretation of the Constitution
- That desegregation could be delayed while public order was at risk
- That enforcing Brown was a matter for Congress under § 5 of the Fourteenth Amendment
E: Cooper declared that "the federal judiciary is supreme in the exposition of the law of the Constitution," binding state officials to Brown.

Q: What is the difference between judicial review and judicial supremacy?
* Judicial review is the power to refuse to apply an unconstitutional law in a case; judicial supremacy is the claim that the Court's interpretations bind everyone
- Judicial review lets courts strike down state laws; judicial supremacy is the further power to strike down acts of Congress, which the Court first claimed in Cooper v. Aaron
- Judicial review is exercised by all courts; judicial supremacy belongs only to the Supreme Court, whose rulings bind lower courts but not the other branches
- Judicial review applies to statutes passed after Marbury; judicial supremacy is the older English idea that courts may void laws contrary to the common law
E: The two ideas come apart. Departmentalists accept judicial review but deny that the other branches must always defer to the Court's reading of the Constitution.

Q: The "counter-majoritarian difficulty," a phrase coined by Alexander Bickel, refers to what problem?
- A narrow majority of Justices can overturn precedents that most Americans support
* Unelected judges can strike down laws passed by elected majorities
- The Senate can block legislation passed by a majority of the House
- A minority of states can block constitutional amendments under Article V
E: Bickel's The Least Dangerous Branch framed the central puzzle of judicial review: how unelected judges can legitimately override democratic majorities.

## guide:interpretation
Q: Which statement best describes original public meaning originalism?
- Constitutional text should be read as the delegates at Philadelphia privately intended it to work
* Constitutional text should be read as a reasonable reader understood it when it was ratified
- Constitutional text should be read as its words are ordinarily understood by readers today
- Constitutional text should be read as the first Congresses applied it in their early statutes
E: Modern originalism focuses on the public meaning of the words at ratification rather than the subjective intentions of the drafters.

Q: Living constitutionalism holds that:
- Constitutional meaning is fixed at ratification, but judges may apply it to new technologies
* Constitutional meaning can develop over time, often through precedent and changing understandings
- Constitutional meaning should be set by the elected branches, with courts deferring to Congress
- Constitutional meaning can change only through Article V amendments that the people approve
E: Living constitutionalists, such as David Strauss in The Living Constitution, emphasize common-law-style development through precedent.

Q: District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) is often cited as an example of:
- A decision resting mainly on the Court's own precedents
* Extensive originalist analysis of a constitutional provision
- Deference to the legislature's judgment about public safety
- A balancing test weighing individual rights against state interests
E: Both Justice Scalia's majority and Justice Stevens's dissent devoted most of their opinions to the historical meaning of the Second Amendment.

Q: In Home Building & Loan v. Blaisdell (1934), the Court upheld a mortgage moratorium despite the Contracts Clause. Why is the case often discussed in debates over interpretation?
- It relied on the framers' private notes to show that the clause was never meant to reach mortgages, a method later rejected by originalists
* It read the clause flexibly in light of an economic emergency, which critics see as departing from original meaning
- It held that the Contracts Clause binds only Congress, reversing a century of cases that had applied it against the states
- It treated the moratorium as a taking of property and required the state to compensate lenders for the payments delayed
E: Blaisdell is a classic example of reading constitutional text in light of changed circumstances, and originalists often criticize it on that ground.

Q: "Stare decisis" refers to:
- The rule that lower courts must follow the latest opinion of any higher court
* The principle that courts should generally follow precedent
- The principle that courts should read statutes to avoid constitutional doubts
- The rule that federal courts follow state courts on questions of state law
E: Stare decisis ("to stand by things decided") promotes stability, though the Court treats it as weaker in constitutional cases.

## guide:incorporation
Q: Barron v. Baltimore (1833) held that the Bill of Rights:
* Limited only the federal government, not the states
- Applied to the states only where a state constitution lacked a similar right
- Bound the states once the Fourteenth Amendment was ratified
- Limited states only in cases involving the taking of property
E: Chief Justice Marshall held the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause did not restrain the city of Baltimore, because the Bill of Rights limited only the national government.

Q: Through which clause has the Court applied most of the Bill of Rights to the states?
- The Privileges or Immunities Clause
* The Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment
- The Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment
- The Supremacy Clause of Article VI
E: After the Slaughter-House Cases narrowed the Privileges or Immunities Clause, the Court used Fourteenth Amendment due process to "incorporate" rights one by one.

Q: McDonald v. City of Chicago (2010) held that:
- The Second Amendment protects the right to bear arms only in connection with service in a state militia
* The Second Amendment right recognized in Heller applies against the states
- Chicago's handgun ban was valid because Heller applied only to federal enclaves like the District of Columbia
- The Second Amendment applies to the states through the Privileges or Immunities Clause, overruling Slaughter-House
E: McDonald incorporated the individual right to keep and bear arms against state and local governments.

Q: Timbs v. Indiana (2019) incorporated which protection?
- The Fifth Amendment right to a grand jury indictment
- The Sixth Amendment right to a unanimous jury
* The Excessive Fines Clause of the Eighth Amendment
- The Seventh Amendment civil jury right
E: Timbs held that the protection against excessive fines, which dates back to Magna Carta, is fundamental and applies to the states.

Q: Which of these has the Supreme Court NOT incorporated against the states?
- The First Amendment freedom of speech
- The Sixth Amendment right to counsel
* The Fifth Amendment requirement of a grand jury indictment
- The Fourth Amendment protection against unreasonable searches
E: Hurtado v. California (1884) held that states need not use grand juries, and that remains the law.

## guide:separation-of-powers
Q: Which branch of the federal government does Article I create?
* Congress
- The President
- The Supreme Court
- The states
E: Article I vests "all legislative Powers herein granted" in a Congress made up of a Senate and a House of Representatives.

Q: In Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952), the Court held that President Truman's seizure of the steel mills was:
- Valid, because his power as commander in chief extended to securing steel for troops in Korea
* Unconstitutional, because the President lacked authority from the Constitution or Congress
- Valid for a limited time, because a wartime emergency allowed temporary seizures with compensation
- A political question, because disputes between the President and industry belong to Congress
E: The seizure was struck down. Justice Jackson's concurrence set out the famous three-category framework for presidential power.

Q: INS v. Chadha (1983) struck down the "legislative veto" because it violated:
- The Due Process Clause, because Chadha had no hearing before the House voted to reverse his suspension
* Bicameralism and presentment, the requirements that laws pass both houses and go to the President
- The Appointments Clause, because members of Congress cannot carry out executive functions like deportation
- The Bill of Attainder Clause, because the House singled out one person for punishment without a trial
E: A one-house resolution overriding an executive decision was lawmaking without passage by both houses and presentment to the President.

Q: Which of these is a "check" the Senate has on the President?
- Overriding his vetoes by a simple majority vote
* Confirming or rejecting nominees and treaties
- Removing his cabinet officers by majority vote
- Starting impeachment proceedings against him
E: The Senate gives "advice and consent" to appointments (by majority) and to treaties (by two-thirds).

Q: Morrison v. Olson (1988) upheld a law that:
* Protected an independent counsel from being fired except for good cause
- Let Congress remove executive officers through a joint resolution
- Gave the President a line-item veto over appropriations bills
- Let the Senate block the firing of any officer it had confirmed
E: The Court found the good-cause removal limit did not unduly interfere with the President's duties. Justice Scalia's lone dissent is now often quoted.

## guide:executive-power
Q: United States v. Nixon (1974) held that executive privilege:
- Is absolute for communications with advisers, but did not cover the tapes because they concerned crimes
* Exists but is qualified, and here yielded to the need for evidence in a criminal trial
- Does not exist under the Constitution, so the President must comply with any valid subpoena
- Belongs to the presidency, so only a sitting President may invoke it, and not a former one
E: The Court ordered President Nixon to produce the White House tapes, which led to his resignation.

Q: In Justice Jackson's Youngstown framework, presidential power is at its "lowest ebb" when the President:
- Acts with the express or implied authorization of Congress
- Acts in an area where Congress has neither granted nor denied authority
* Acts contrary to the express or implied will of Congress
- Acts in foreign affairs without a treaty or statute to rely on
E: At the lowest ebb, the President can rely only on his own constitutional powers minus whatever powers Congress has over the matter.

Q: Trump v. United States (2024) addressed whether a former President:
- Can be barred from the ballot for insurrection
* Has immunity from criminal prosecution for official acts
- Can be impeached and tried by the Senate after leaving office
- Can be sued for damages over his official acts
E: The Court held that a former President has absolute immunity for core constitutional acts, at least presumptive immunity for other official acts, and none for unofficial acts.

Q: The "unitary executive" theory holds that:
* The President must be able to control, including by removing, officers who execute federal law
- The President and Vice President share the executive power, each with authority over half the agencies
- Executive power is unified in the federal government, so states may not enforce federal law on their own
- Congress may create independent agencies, but only if a single officer heads each one and reports to it
E: The theory draws on Article II's Vesting Clause, which vests "the executive Power" in a single President.

Q: Which power does the President NOT have under Article II?
- Granting pardons for federal offenses
- Making treaties with Senate consent
* Declaring war
- Nominating federal judges
E: Article I gives Congress the power to declare war. The President is commander in chief of the armed forces.

## guide:federalism
Q: McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) held that:
* Congress could create a national bank, and Maryland could not tax it
- Congress could create a national bank, but Maryland could tax its notes
- Congress lacked power to create a bank, since no clause names one
- Maryland could tax the bank because states share the taxing power
E: Marshall read the Necessary and Proper Clause broadly and declared that "the power to tax involves the power to destroy."

Q: The Tenth Amendment provides that:
- Federal law prevails over state law when the two conflict
* Powers not delegated to the United States are reserved to the states or the people
- Rights not listed in the Constitution are retained by the people
- States may not be sued by citizens of other states in federal court without consent
E: The Tenth Amendment confirms the principle of enumerated federal powers.

Q: Printz v. United States (1997) held that Congress may not:
- Regulate the sale of handguns through federal background checks
* Command state officers to carry out a federal regulatory program
- Attach conditions to federal grants for local law enforcement
- Preempt state gun laws that are stricter than federal law
E: Requiring local sheriffs to run background checks under the Brady Act violated the "anti-commandeering" principle.

Q: New York v. United States (1992) struck down a provision that:
- Banned states from accepting low-level radioactive waste from other states
* Required states to take title to radioactive waste or enact federal regulations
- Taxed states that shipped radioactive waste to other states
- Let states charge rising fees on out-of-state radioactive waste
E: Congress cannot compel state legislatures to enact or administer a federal program. It can offer incentives or regulate directly.

Q: In the American system, the general "police power" to regulate health, safety and welfare belongs primarily to:
- Congress
* The states
- The President
- The federal courts
E: The national government has only enumerated powers. States keep a general power to legislate for the public good.

## guide:commerce-clause
Q: Gibbons v. Ogden (1824) defined "commerce" to include:
- Only the buying and selling of goods
* Commercial intercourse, including navigation
- Manufacturing and farming within a state
- Only trade with foreign nations
E: Marshall rejected a narrow "traffic" definition. Commerce, he said, "is intercourse," which includes navigation between states.

Q: In Wickard v. Filburn (1942), a farmer was penalized for growing wheat that:
- He sold to buyers in other states
* He grew for use on his own farm
- He sold to a federal grain program
- He shipped to mills in another state
E: The Court aggregated the effect of all such home-grown wheat on the national market. That substantial effect justified federal regulation.

Q: United States v. Lopez (1995) was significant because:
* It was the first time in decades the Court held that a law exceeded the commerce power
- It held for the first time that Congress may regulate activities that substantially affect commerce
- It upheld a federal gun law by aggregating the effects of guns in schools across the country
- It held that Congress may regulate schools under the commerce power but not under the spending power
E: The Gun-Free School Zones Act regulated noneconomic activity with only an attenuated connection to interstate commerce.

Q: Which is NOT one of the three categories of activity Congress may regulate under the Commerce Clause?
- Channels of interstate commerce
- Instrumentalities of interstate commerce
- Activities that substantially affect interstate commerce
* Any activity Congress finds morally harmful
E: Lopez identified the three categories. Congress has no general police power.

Q: In NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate was ultimately upheld under:
- The Commerce Clause
* The taxing power
- The Necessary and Proper Clause alone
- The Spending Clause
E: Five Justices said the Commerce Clause does not let Congress compel people to buy insurance. A different five upheld the mandate's penalty as a tax.

## guide:standing
Q: What are the three elements of constitutional standing under Lujan v. Defenders of Wildlife?
* Injury in fact, causation, and redressability
- Injury in fact, ripeness, and a live controversy
- Causation, adverseness, and a final judgment
- Redressability, finality, and a cause of action
E: The plaintiff must show a concrete and particularized injury, fairly traceable to the defendant, that a court decision would likely redress.

Q: Why do federal courts require standing?
- Because the Due Process Clause guarantees defendants notice of a specific claim against them
* Because Article III limits federal courts to deciding actual "cases" and "controversies"
- Because the Judiciary Act of 1789 limited suits to parties with a financial stake in the outcome
- Because the Eleventh Amendment bars suits brought on behalf of the general public interest
E: Standing keeps courts from issuing advisory opinions and keeps them within the judicial role.

Q: Massachusetts v. EPA (2007) gave states "special solicitude" in standing analysis. What was the dispute about?
- EPA's approval of offshore drilling near the coast
* EPA's refusal to regulate greenhouse gas emissions from vehicles
- EPA's limits on mercury emissions from power plants
- EPA's rules for cleaning up polluted rivers and state waterways
E: The Court found Massachusetts had standing based on harm to its coastline from climate change.

Q: A citizen sues because she believes a federal program is wasteful but suffers no personal harm. A court will most likely:
- Hear the case, because every taxpayer has standing to challenge how federal money is spent
* Dismiss for lack of standing because it is a generalized grievance
- Dismiss it as moot, because the program's funds have already been spent
- Hear the case, but only if she first asked Congress to stop the program
E: Generalized grievances shared by all citizens do not satisfy Article III's requirement of a particularized injury.

Q: "Mootness" means a case:
- Was filed before any injury had actually occurred
* No longer presents a live controversy
- Raises a question committed to another branch
- Was brought by a party who suffered no injury
E: If events resolve the dispute while the case is pending, it becomes moot. There are exceptions, such as for disputes "capable of repetition, yet evading review."

## guide:administrative-state
Q: The "nondelegation doctrine" concerns:
* Whether Congress may hand off its lawmaking power to agencies
- Whether agencies may hand off rulemaking to private industry groups
- Whether the President may refuse to enforce laws Congress passes
- Whether Congress may give courts the power to write procedural rules
E: Article I vests legislative power in Congress. The Court allows delegation only with an "intelligible principle" to guide the agency.

Q: Chevron v. NRDC (1984) instructed courts to:
- Read ambiguous statutes in favor of the regulated party
* Defer to reasonable agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes
- Defer to agency interpretations only after notice-and-comment
- Decide the best reading of a statute without agency input
E: Chevron deference governed for 40 years until Loper Bright overruled it in 2024.

Q: Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo (2024) held that:
- Agencies must obtain clear congressional authorization before regulating any major industry
* Courts must exercise independent judgment about what statutes mean, without Chevron deference
- Agency rules are invalid unless Congress approves them by joint resolution within 60 days
- Courts must defer to agency interpretations only when the agency has special expertise
E: Relying on the Administrative Procedure Act and the judicial role, the Court overruled Chevron.

Q: Under the "major questions doctrine," as in West Virginia v. EPA (2022):
* Agencies need clear congressional authorization to decide issues of vast economic and political significance
- Courts decide the meaning of statutes on major questions, but defer to agencies on minor technical questions
- Congress must give an intelligible principle when it delegates power over questions of national importance
- Agencies may decide major questions only after holding public hearings and consulting the affected states
E: The Court held EPA lacked clear authority to restructure the power sector by shifting generation away from coal.

Q: The phrase "intelligible principle" comes from which case?
- Schechter Poultry Corp. v. United States (1935)
* J.W. Hampton, Jr. & Co. v. United States (1928)
- Panama Refining Co. v. Ryan (1935)
- Whitman v. American Trucking Ass'ns (2001)
E: J.W. Hampton upheld a tariff delegation and supplied the test that has governed delegation ever since.

## guide:free-speech
Q: Under Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), speech advocating illegal action can be punished only if it is:
- A clear and present danger of a substantive evil that Congress has a right to prevent
* Directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it
- Advocacy of the forcible overthrow of the government, rather than abstract teaching
- Likely to provoke the average listener to fight, under the fighting words doctrine
E: Brandenburg protected a Klan leader's speech and set the modern incitement test.

Q: New York Times v. Sullivan (1964) requires a public official suing for defamation to prove:
- That the statement was false and that the paper failed to check it with reasonable care
* "Actual malice": knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth
- That the statement was false and caused actual harm to his reputation in the community
- "Specific intent": that the paper published the statement in order to injure him
E: The rule protects robust debate about public officials, even when it includes some errors.

Q: Tinker v. Des Moines (1969) held that students wearing black armbands to protest the Vietnam War:
- Could be disciplined, because schools may forbid political symbols that other students find offensive
* Were protected because their speech did not substantially disrupt school
- Were protected only because the armbands were silent, while spoken protest could be banned at school
- Could be disciplined, because the school's rule was adopted before the protest and applied to everyone
E: Students do not "shed their constitutional rights … at the schoolhouse gate."

Q: A law banning all signs that criticize the mayor is an example of:
- A content-neutral time, place and manner rule
* Viewpoint discrimination
- A permissible regulation of government speech
- A prior restraint subject to intermediate scrutiny
E: Targeting speech because of the speaker's opinion is the most disfavored form of content discrimination.

Q: Which of these categories is NOT protected by the First Amendment?
- Flag burning as political protest
- Offensive or profane political speech
* True threats of violence
- Hate speech that does not threaten or incite
E: True threats are unprotected. Flag burning (Texas v. Johnson) and offensive political speech are protected.

## guide:religion
Q: The two religion clauses of the First Amendment are:
* The Establishment Clause and the Free Exercise Clause
- The Free Exercise Clause and the Religious Test Clause
- The Establishment Clause and the Free Speech Clause
- The Free Exercise Clause and the Equal Protection Clause
E: Congress may make no law "respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof."

Q: Employment Division v. Smith (1990) held that:
- Laws burdening religious practice must pass strict scrutiny
* Neutral, generally applicable laws need not exempt religious practices
- States must exempt sacramental drug use from criminal laws
- Religious exemptions from neutral laws violate the Establishment Clause
E: Smith denied an exemption for sacramental peyote use. Congress responded with the Religious Freedom Restoration Act (RFRA).

Q: Kennedy v. Bremerton School District (2022) involved:
- A teacher leading students in prayer in class
* A football coach praying at midfield after games
- A school district paying for chaplains at games
- A student giving a prayer over the loudspeaker
E: The Court held the coach's prayer was protected, and replaced the Lemon test with a focus on historical practices and understandings.

Q: Which phrase did Thomas Jefferson famously use to describe the Establishment Clause?
- "No religious test shall ever be required"
* "A wall of separation between church and state"
- "The free exercise of religion according to conscience"
- "A hedge between the garden and the wilderness"
E: Jefferson's 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists, later quoted in Everson v. Board of Education (1947).

Q: Under current law, a law that targets a particular religious practice must satisfy:
- Rational basis review
* Strict scrutiny
- The Lemon test
- Intermediate scrutiny
E: Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah (1993) struck down ordinances aimed at animal sacrifice by Santería practitioners.

## guide:second-amendment
Q: District of Columbia v. Heller (2008) held that the Second Amendment protects:
- A right to keep and bear arms tied to service in a state militia, including a militia member's own rifle
* An individual right to keep and bear arms, including handguns in the home for self-defense
- An individual right to carry handguns in public for self-defense, which states may not condition on need
- An individual right to keep any weapon in common use, including military rifles, free from licensing laws
E: Heller struck down D.C.'s handgun ban and noted that the right is "not unlimited."

Q: New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen (2022) requires a gun regulation to be:
- Substantially related to an important interest in public safety
* Consistent with the nation's historical tradition of firearm regulation
- Supported by evidence that it reduces gun violence in practice
- Narrowly tailored to a compelling interest in preventing crime
E: Bruen replaced means-end scrutiny with a test based on text, history and tradition.

Q: United States v. Rahimi (2024) upheld a federal law disarming people who:
* Are subject to domestic-violence restraining orders
- Have been convicted of nonviolent felonies
- Are unlawful users of marijuana or other controlled substances
- Have been committed to a mental institution
E: The Court found the law consistent with historical traditions of disarming people who pose a credible threat to others.

Q: Which case applied the Second Amendment to state and local governments?
- District of Columbia v. Heller
* McDonald v. City of Chicago
- New York State Rifle & Pistol Ass'n v. Bruen
- United States v. Miller
E: McDonald (2010) incorporated the right through the Fourteenth Amendment.

Q: Heller said which of the following kinds of laws are "presumptively lawful"?
- Requirements that applicants show a special need before receiving a license to carry
* Prohibitions on possession by felons and in sensitive places like schools
- Bans on handguns in the home in cities with high rates of gun violence
- Requirements that guns at home be kept disassembled or fitted with trigger locks
E: The Court listed "longstanding prohibitions" that its decision did not cast doubt on.

## guide:equal-protection
Q: Brown v. Board of Education (1954) held that:
* Racially segregated public schools are inherently unequal
- Segregated schools are valid if they are truly equal in funding
- Segregation in schools is valid but not in public transportation
- States must bus students to achieve racial balance in schools
E: Brown overruled Plessy v. Ferguson in public education. Brown II (1955) ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed."

Q: Which level of scrutiny applies to government classifications based on race?
- Rational basis
- Intermediate scrutiny
* Strict scrutiny
- Rational basis with bite
E: Racial classifications must be narrowly tailored to a compelling government interest.

Q: Loving v. Virginia (1967) struck down:
- Laws segregating public schools
* Laws banning interracial marriage
- Poll taxes in state elections
- Literacy tests for voting
E: Virginia's argument that the law applied equally to both races failed. The racial classification itself was the problem.

Q: Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard (2023) held that:
* Harvard's and UNC's race-conscious admissions programs violated equal protection
- Universities may consider race as one factor if they set a date to stop doing so
- Harvard's program was valid, but UNC's violated equal protection as a state school
- Admissions programs may use race to remedy a university's own past discrimination only
E: The programs lacked measurable goals and a logical end point, and they used race as a negative.

Q: Sex-based classifications receive which level of scrutiny?
- Rational basis, requiring only a legitimate interest and a rational connection
* Intermediate scrutiny, requiring an "exceedingly persuasive justification"
- Strict scrutiny, requiring narrow tailoring to a compelling interest
- Rational basis with bite, requiring proof that the law reflects no animus
E: Craig v. Boren (1976) and United States v. Virginia (1996) set the standard.

## guide:due-process
Q: "Substantive due process" protects:
- Fair procedures, such as notice and a hearing, before the government takes life, liberty or property
* Certain fundamental liberties from government interference, regardless of the procedures used
- Equal treatment of similarly situated people, so that laws do not single out disfavored groups
- The rights listed in the Bill of Rights, and only those, against interference by the states
E: Substantive due process is the doctrine behind rights to marry, to raise one's children, and to make certain intimate decisions.

Q: Griswold v. Connecticut (1965) recognized a right of married couples to:
* Use contraception
- Obtain a divorce
- Adopt children
- Educate children at home
E: Justice Douglas found the right in the "penumbras" of specific Bill of Rights guarantees.

Q: Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022) held that:
- The Constitution protects a right to abortion only until fetal viability
* The Constitution does not confer a right to abortion, overruling Roe and Casey
- States may restrict abortion only if the restriction is not an undue burden
- Abortion is protected by equal protection rather than by due process
E: Dobbs applied Glucksberg's history-and-tradition test and returned abortion regulation to elected bodies.

Q: Under Washington v. Glucksberg (1997), an unenumerated right is fundamental only if it is:
- Central to personal dignity and autonomy, as reflected in the Court's evolving precedents
* Deeply rooted in the nation's history and tradition and implicit in ordered liberty
- Recognized by most states today and consistent with contemporary standards of decency
- Closely related to a right expressly listed in the Bill of Rights, within its penumbra
E: Glucksberg rejected a claimed right to physician-assisted suicide under this test.

Q: Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) held that:
* The Fourteenth Amendment requires states to license and recognize same-sex marriages
- States must recognize same-sex marriages from other states but need not license them
- The federal government must recognize same-sex marriages that a state has licensed
- The Equal Protection Clause alone requires states to license same-sex marriages
E: The Court relied on both due process and equal protection.

## guide:fourth-amendment
Q: The Fourth Amendment protects against:
* Unreasonable searches and seizures
- Compelled self-incrimination
- Excessive bail and fines
- Being tried twice for one offense
E: It also requires warrants to be supported by probable cause and to describe particularly what is to be searched.

Q: Mapp v. Ohio (1961) applied which rule to the states?
- The requirement that police warn suspects of their rights before questioning them
* The exclusionary rule, barring illegally obtained evidence from trial
- The requirement that police obtain a warrant before any arrest in a public place
- The rule that defendants may sue officers for damages after an illegal search
E: Mapp extended the exclusionary rule from Weeks v. United States (1914) to state prosecutions.

Q: Katz v. United States (1967) held that the Fourth Amendment protects:
- Only places where the government physically intrudes, such as homes and offices
* People, not places, including conversations where there is a reasonable expectation of privacy
- Papers and effects, but not conversations, which have no physical existence
- Telephone calls made from home, but not those made from public places like phone booths
E: Wiretapping a public phone booth was a search. Justice Harlan's "reasonable expectation of privacy" test came from his concurrence.

Q: Carpenter v. United States (2018) held that police generally need a warrant to obtain:
- Records of bank deposits
* Historical cell-site location records
- Records of numbers dialed
- Trash bags left out at the curb for pickup
E: The Court declined to extend the "third-party doctrine" to the detailed location records phones generate.

Q: The founders' hostility to "general warrants" and "writs of assistance" helped produce which amendment?
- The First
* The Fourth
- The Fifth
- The Ninth
E: General warrants let officers search wherever they pleased. The Fourth Amendment requires particularity.

## guide:trial-rights
Q: Gideon v. Wainwright (1963) held that:
* States must provide counsel to defendants charged with felonies who cannot afford a lawyer
- States must provide counsel only when special circumstances, such as illiteracy, make it necessary
- Defendants have a right to counsel at trial but not at a guilty plea or sentencing hearing
- States must provide counsel to all defendants, including those charged with minor fines
E: Clarence Earl Gideon's handwritten petition led the Court to overrule Betts v. Brady.

Q: Miranda v. Arizona (1966) requires police to warn suspects of their rights before:
- Any questioning at all
* Custodial interrogation
- Making an arrest
- A traffic stop
E: The warnings protect the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination in the coercive setting of custody.

Q: The Sixth Amendment's Confrontation Clause, as interpreted in Crawford v. Washington (2004), bars:
- All out-of-court statements offered for their truth, unless they fall within a recognized hearsay exception and bear adequate indicia of reliability
* Testimonial statements of a witness who does not testify, unless the witness is unavailable and the defendant had a prior chance to cross-examine
- Any statement made to police by a witness who does not testify, unless the trial judge finds it was made under circumstances guaranteeing its truthfulness
- Statements of co-defendants who invoke their privilege not to testify, unless the jury is instructed to consider them only against the speaker
E: Crawford tied the clause to its historical core: preventing trial by affidavit.

Q: Which amendment protects against being "twice put in jeopardy" for the same offense?
- The Fourth
* The Fifth
- The Sixth
- The Eighth
E: The Double Jeopardy Clause is in the Fifth Amendment, alongside the privilege against self-incrimination and the grand jury requirement.

Q: Ramos v. Louisiana (2020) held that:
* Conviction for a serious offense requires a unanimous jury, in state and federal court
- Federal convictions require a unanimous jury, but states may accept 10–2 verdicts
- Juries in serious cases must have twelve members, in state and federal court
- Non-unanimous verdicts are valid if the state constitution allowed them before 1868
E: Ramos overruled Apodaca v. Oregon and ended non-unanimous verdicts in Louisiana and Oregon.

## guide:punishment
Q: The Eighth Amendment prohibits:
* Cruel and unusual punishments, excessive bail, and excessive fines
- Cruel and unusual punishments, double jeopardy, and bills of attainder
- Excessive bail, unreasonable searches, and compelled testimony
- Excessive fines, ex post facto laws, and unanimous-jury waivers
E: The text was drawn almost word for word from the English Bill of Rights of 1689.

Q: Gregg v. Georgia (1976) held that:
- The death penalty violates the Eighth Amendment whenever juries have unguided discretion to impose it
* The death penalty is not per se unconstitutional when imposed under guided-discretion procedures
- The death penalty must be mandatory for certain crimes, so that it is applied without arbitrariness
- The death penalty is constitutional only for murders committed during another felony like robbery
E: Gregg ended the four-year moratorium that followed Furman v. Georgia (1972).

Q: Roper v. Simmons (2005) held that the death penalty may not be imposed on:
- Offenders with an intellectual disability
* Offenders who were under 18 when they committed the crime
- Offenders convicted of crimes other than murder
- Offenders who were under 21 at the time of their trial
E: The Court relied on "evolving standards of decency" and national consensus.

Q: The phrase "evolving standards of decency that mark the progress of a maturing society" comes from:
* Trop v. Dulles (1958)
- Furman v. Georgia (1972)
- Weems v. United States (1910)
- Coker v. Georgia (1977)
E: Trop struck down revoking citizenship as a punishment for desertion. The phrase became the touchstone of Eighth Amendment analysis.

Q: Timbs v. Indiana (2019) involved the forfeiture of:
- A house used to store drugs, worth less than the fine
* A Land Rover worth far more than the maximum fine for the drug offense
- Cash found in a car, which the state could not tie to any crime
- A small business whose owner failed to pay state sales taxes
E: The Court held that the Excessive Fines Clause applies to the states.

## guide:takings
Q: The Takings Clause requires that private property taken for public use be accompanied by:
* Just compensation
- A jury trial
- A legislative finding
- The owner's consent
E: The clause does not forbid takings. It conditions them on payment.

Q: Kelo v. City of New London (2005) held that "public use" includes:
- Only uses the public can physically enter, such as roads, parks and government buildings
* Economic development under a comprehensive plan, even when the land goes to private developers
- Transfers to private parties only when the land is blighted and harms the neighborhood around it
- Any purpose a city declares, without review, because public use is a political question
E: The decision prompted widespread state reforms limiting eminent domain.

Q: Penn Central v. New York City (1978) considered whether a historic landmark designation that barred a skyscraper above Grand Central Terminal was a taking. The Court:
- Held it was a taking, because the air rights above the station were property
* Held it was not a taking, using a balancing test
- Held it was a per se taking, because it denied all use of the air space
- Held landmark laws are valid only if owners receive transferable rights
E: Penn Central's factors (economic impact, investment-backed expectations, and the character of the government action) govern most regulatory takings claims.

Q: Under Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992), a regulation is a per se taking when it:
* Deprives land of all economically beneficial use
- Reduces the land's value by more than half
- Bars the owner from building any residence
- Was adopted after the owner bought the land
E: The exception is uses already prohibited by background principles of nuisance or property law.

Q: A permanent physical occupation of property authorized by the government, such as a cable box on a building, is:
- A taking only if it substantially reduces the building's value
* A taking regardless of how small
- Never a taking if it serves a public purpose like communication
- A taking only if the owner did not consent to the cable service
E: Loretto v. Teleprompter (1982); compare Cedar Point Nursery v. Hassid (2021).

## guide:voting
Q: Baker v. Carr (1962) held that challenges to legislative apportionment:
- Are political questions under the Guarantee Clause
* Are justiciable in federal court
- Must first be brought in state court
- Apply only to congressional districts
E: Baker opened the courthouse doors to malapportionment claims.

Q: The principle of "one person, one vote" requires:
* Legislative districts to have roughly equal populations
- Every eligible citizen to have one registration and one ballot
- Legislative districts to have roughly equal numbers of voters
- Each county to have one seat in the state senate
E: Reynolds v. Sims (1964) applied it to both houses of state legislatures: "Legislators represent people, not trees or acres."

Q: Shelby County v. Holder (2013) struck down:
- The Voting Rights Act's ban on voting practices that have a racially discriminatory result
* The coverage formula that determined which jurisdictions needed federal preclearance
- The preclearance requirement itself, holding that Congress can never require it
- The Voting Rights Act's ban on literacy tests as a condition of registering to vote
E: The Court held the formula relied on decades-old data and violated the states' equal sovereignty.

Q: Rucho v. Common Cause (2019) held that partisan gerrymandering claims:
- Are justiciable but must show intent, effect and no other justification
* Present political questions beyond the reach of federal courts
- Are justiciable only under the Voting Rights Act, not the Constitution
- May be brought only by political parties, not by individual voters
E: The Court found no judicially manageable standard. State courts and legislatures can still address the problem.

Q: Which amendment prohibits denying the vote based on race?
- The Thirteenth
- The Fourteenth
* The Fifteenth
- The Nineteenth
E: Ratified in 1870. The Nineteenth Amendment (1920) prohibits sex discrimination in voting.

## guide:war-powers
Q: Who has the constitutional power to declare war?
* Congress
- The President
- The Senate alone
- The House alone
E: Article I, Section 8 gives Congress the power "To declare War." The President is commander in chief.

Q: The War Powers Resolution of 1973 requires the President to:
- Obtain a declaration of war before sending troops into hostilities for more than 30 days
* Report to Congress within 48 hours and end unauthorized hostilities within 60–90 days
- Consult congressional leaders before any action and end hostilities if either House objects
- Obtain authorization from Congress within 10 days or withdraw forces immediately
E: Presidents of both parties have questioned its constitutionality.

Q: Hamdi v. Rumsfeld (2004) held that a U.S. citizen detained as an enemy combatant:
- May be held without a hearing for as long as the conflict lasts, because Congress authorized force
* Is entitled to notice and a meaningful opportunity to contest the detention before a neutral decisionmaker
- Must be charged with a crime and tried in civilian court, or released within a reasonable time
- May challenge the detention only before a military commission, whose decision is final and unreviewable
E: Justice O'Connor's plurality wrote that "a state of war is not a blank check for the President."

Q: Boumediene v. Bush (2008) held that detainees at Guantanamo Bay:
* Have the constitutional privilege of habeas corpus
- Have only the review that Congress provides by statute
- Have rights under the Geneva Conventions alone
- Must be tried in military commissions within a year
E: The Military Commissions Act's elimination of habeas jurisdiction was an unconstitutional suspension of the writ.

Q: The Suspension Clause provides that the writ of habeas corpus may be suspended only:
- By the President, when he finds that a rebellion threatens the safety of the capital
* When in cases of rebellion or invasion the public safety may require it
- By Congress, during a declared war, for no more than sixty days at a time
- When the Supreme Court certifies that civilian courts cannot safely sit in an area
E: Article I, Section 9. Lincoln's 1861 suspension without Congress was famously challenged in Ex parte Merryman.
